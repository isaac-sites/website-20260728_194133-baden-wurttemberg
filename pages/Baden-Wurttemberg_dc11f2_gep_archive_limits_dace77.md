## Introduction

The GEP archive is the most useful long-term public record for tracing reported UFO and unidentified aerial phenomenon cases connected with Baden-Württemberg. It preserves dates, locations, witness summaries, classifications and investigative outcomes across more than five decades. In a country without a comprehensive official state or federal sighting archive, that continuity makes the collection unusually valuable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/15882235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2025.06 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Baden-Wurttemberg_dc11f2_gep_archive_limits_dace77-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for GEP Archive" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

It is not, however, a census of everything unusual seen over Baden-Württemberg. The Society for the Investigation of the UFO Phenomenon, known by its German initials GEP, records cases that reach a private voluntary organisation through email, forms, telephone calls, social media, referrals and other channels. The resulting dataset shows what was reported to GEP and how GEP assessed it—not the true number of sightings, their geographical prevalence or the proportion of all aerial events that remain unexplained.

## What the archive contains

GEP has collected reports since 1972. Its openly downloadable dataset includes fields for the case number, sighting date and time, country, federal state, postcode, location, reporting route, a free-text account, descriptive classifications and the eventual identification or investigative result. Personal details of [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses-afe9a0/' | relative_url }}) are removed for data-protection reasons. The public release available through Zenodo covers thousands of entries and is supplied as a machine-readable CSV file, allowing searches by place, date, classification and proposed explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/15882235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2025.06 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>

For research into Baden-Württemberg, this structure is more useful than a folder of undigested newspaper cuttings. A reader can distinguish a report made in Stuttgart from one in Radolfzell, see whether it arrived promptly or years after the event, and check whether investigators associated it with a balloon, aircraft, planet, satellite, light effect or some other ordinary source. The record also preserves cases that have not yet reached a conclusion, making it possible to separate “currently unresolved” from “investigated and judged genuinely anomalous”.

The archive records the evolution of reporting technology as well as the sightings themselves. Older files were commonly built from letters, telephone interviews and paper questionnaires. Recent entries may begin with an online database form, an email, a social-media message, photographs or mobile-phone video. GEP’s published monthly case lists show that these are initial submissions: investigation, discussion and final classification can occur later, sometimes followed by a fuller account in its specialist journal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/ufo-meldungen/19-neue-ufo_meldungen-im-juni-2022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">19 neue ufo meldungen im juni 2022</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falldatenbank, 28.05.2022)... That distinction is important when reading recent Baden-Württemberg entries. For example, the June 2025 dataset records a May report from Stuttgart concerning a bright, reflective object filmed in daylight. GEP listed a foil balloon as the favoured explanation. An April report from Radolfzell, by contrast, initially contained little more than a photograph of a bright light and remained too sparsely described for an immediate conclusion. These entries do not carry equal evidential weight merely because both appear in the same database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/15882235/files/UFO-UAP-Falldaten_GEP_1972-2025.06.csv?download=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO UAP Falldaten GEP 1972 2025.06.csv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">zenodo.org...</span></span></span>

## How reports enter the collection

The archive begins with self-selection. Someone must notice an object or light, regard it as sufficiently unusual, learn that GEP exists and decide to make contact. The organisation may then request a structured questionnaire, original photographs or video, directional information, weather details, witness accounts and other material. GEP researchers describe their approach as case-based citizen science, using witness interviews, document checks, image analysis and comparisons with astronomical, aviation and technological sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/ta-guenter/pdf/JAnom23-2_302_Ammon_etal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[ufo-forschung.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JAnom23 2 302 Ammon etal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JAnom23 2 302 Ammon etal</span></span></span>

Reports can also arrive after referral by another body. GEP material notes links with organisations including German air-navigation services and the University of Würzburg’s interdisciplinary extraterrestrial-research centre. Such referrals can strengthen the archive’s practical role as a national contact point, but they do not turn it into an official reporting system. Police forces, airports, military units, newspapers, other private UFO groups and individual witnesses may retain separate information that never enters the GEP collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesellschaft_zur_Erforschung_des_UFO-Ph%C3%A4nomens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens</span></span></span>

Timing affects the quality of what reaches the archive. A prompt report may allow investigators to reconstruct aircraft movements, satellite passes, astronomical positions or public events. A recollection submitted decades later may still be historically interesting, but precise checks become harder. Human memory can preserve the emotional impact of an experience while losing exact bearings, durations, angular sizes and sequences—details that often decide whether an apparent anomaly can be identified.

The reporting route also changes what is selected. A conspicuous photograph may be submitted even when nobody noticed anything unusual at the time; the “object” may later prove to be an insect, reflection or image artefact. Conversely, a pilot or driver may experience something striking but take no photograph because attention was directed towards safety. The database therefore mixes direct visual sightings, retrospective discoveries in images, unusual personal experiences and requests for help identifying ordinary-looking lights.

## What the classifications really mean

The archive becomes most valuable after investigation, because it does not treat every unexplained observation as evidence of an extraordinary vehicle. GEP classifications include identified objects, reports lacking sufficient information and cases for which no satisfactory explanation was established. Its published descriptions indicate that conventional explanations account for the large majority of investigated submissions, while smaller groups remain unresolved or cannot be assessed adequately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesellschaft_zur_Erforschung_des_UFO-Ph%C3%A4nomens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens</span></span></span>

These categories answer different questions:

* **Identified cases** have been matched with a plausible source such as an aircraft, balloon, satellite, star, planet, meteor, drone, lantern or projected light.
* **Insufficient-data cases** cannot be tested properly because crucial information is missing, contradictory or no longer obtainable.
* **[Unresolved cases]({{ 'unresolved-cases-25749b/' | relative_url }})** have survived the investigation undertaken, but this does not establish that the object was exotic or beyond known technology.
* **Open or provisional entries** may simply be awaiting further work.

A high number of unresolved records can therefore reflect poor information rather than strong anomalies. Canada’s official Sky Canada review reached the same general methodological conclusion when examining public-reporting systems: many cases remain inconclusive because descriptions are too vague or supporting data are absent. “Unexplained” and “inadequately documented” must not be merged into a single mystery category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[Science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial</span></span></span>

The archive also permits later correction. A case initially described as unidentified may become explainable when launch records, flight tracking, astronomical software or better imagery become available. Conversely, a confident early explanation may need revision if it fails to match the time, direction or observed behaviour. A database that preserves both the original claim and the investigative outcome is therefore more informative than a simple list of alleged sightings.

## Biases and gaps in the record

The largest limitation is **reporting bias**. GEP cannot record sightings that people keep to themselves, report only to police, send to another UFO organisation or describe solely in local media. Publicity can sharply increase submissions by making witnesses aware of a reporting centre or prompting them to reinterpret earlier observations. A quiet year in the database may mean fewer sightings, lower public interest, weaker publicity or reduced investigative capacity; the archive alone cannot determine which.

This is visible in the contrast between organisations. SWR reported that the Central Research Network for Extraordinary Sky Phenomena, or CENAP, received more than 150 submissions from Baden-Württemberg during 2024. That total cannot simply be added to or directly compared with GEP figures. Some events may have been reported to both groups, while others reached only one. Different organisations also use different intake practices, categories and thresholds for combining multiple witnesses into a single event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swr.de/swraktuell/baden-wuerttemberg/rekord-ufo-meldungen-100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swr.de">[SWR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Sichtungen in Deutschland auf Rekord-Niveau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Sichtungen in Deutschland auf Rekord-Niveau</span></span></span>

There is also **population and access bias**. More reports can be expected from places with more residents, more internet use, greater [media coverage]({{ 'media-coverage/' | relative_url }}) and easier awareness of GEP. Stuttgart and other urban areas generate many observers but also contain dense air traffic, illuminated advertising, construction lighting, drones and other potential sources of misidentification. Rural areas offer darker skies but fewer possible reporters. Raw map clusters cannot therefore be read straightforwardly as concentrations of anomalous activity.

**Technology bias** changes the archive over time. [Sky lanterns]({{ 'sky-lanterns/' | relative_url }}) affected reports during one period; drones and satellite constellations became prominent later. Mobile phones have greatly increased the number of images submitted, yet a phone video of a distant point of light rarely supplies reliable distance, speed or size. Modern imagery increases the quantity of records without automatically improving their evidential quality. The public dataset itself demonstrates the growing presence of reports involving drones, foil balloons, aircraft, image artefacts and bright astronomical objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/15882235/files/UFO-UAP-Falldaten_GEP_1972-2025.06.csv?download=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO UAP Falldaten GEP 1972 2025.06.csv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">zenodo.org...</span></span></span>

**Investigator and classification bias** must also be considered. Case assessment involves judgement: whether witness information is sufficient, which explanation best fits and how strongly that identification should be expressed. GEP uses formal categories and publishes methodological material, which improves transparency, but it remains a privately operated archive maintained largely through citizen-science work rather than a uniformly funded state system. Changes in personnel, available software, investigation time and classification rules may affect comparisons across decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380530617_UAP_Research_in_Germany_Single_Case_Studies_Data_Management_Understanding_of_Strangeness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Finally, the public dataset is necessarily thinner than the underlying case files. Witness identities are withheld, and sensitive correspondence, full questionnaires, original-resolution media or investigator notes may not be included in the downloadable table. Anonymisation is ethically necessary, but it can prevent outside researchers from independently checking a witness’s background, contacting additional observers or reconstructing every investigative decision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/15882235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2025.06 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>

## What the archive can and cannot show


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Used carefully, the GEP archive can show which kinds of objects repeatedly generate reports in Baden-Württemberg, how submission rates change, where documented cases occurred and how investigators classified them. It can identify promising individual cases for deeper research and reveal recurring failure modes, such as missing times, uncertain directions, overestimated speed or distant lights recorded without reference points.

</div>

It cannot reliably measure the incidence of unexplained aerial phenomena in the state. There is no known denominator: researchers do not know how many people were looking at the sky, how many unusual events occurred or what proportion were reported. Academic studies using other public UFO-reporting databases have similarly found that recorded distributions are affected by population, sky visibility, season, environmental conditions and the behaviour of reporters. Such datasets describe submissions, not a controlled survey of the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

Nor can the archive by itself prove that Baden-Württemberg has a genuine hotspot, a historic flap or a connection with a particular airport or military site. A cluster near Stuttgart might reflect population and air traffic; a rise after a widely visible satellite launch may record one event reported by numerous people; a gap in an earlier decade may reflect lost or unsubmitted material. Claims of patterns require comparison with population data, aviation activity, visibility conditions, media coverage and records held elsewhere.

The most defensible use of the archive is therefore case-led rather than sensationally statistical. A strong entry is one with prompt reporting, several independent witnesses, accurate times and directions, original imagery, instrument data or checkable aviation and astronomical context. A weak entry may remain formally unidentified while consisting only of a delayed memory or an unstructured description of a light. The database allows both to be found, but the reader must not treat them as equivalent.

## The archive’s place in Baden-Württemberg’s UFO history

GEP’s importance arises partly from an institutional absence. German authorities have not maintained a public, comprehensive state-by-state UFO investigation archive comparable with France’s official system. Bundestag research and federal parliamentary answers have recorded the government’s position that it did not possess a general body of files documenting UFO or extraterrestrial sightings, although unfamiliar aircraft could still attract attention under aviation or security responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/406336/WD-8-104-09-pdf.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundestag.de">[Deutscher Bundestag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundestag.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deutscher BundestagWissenschaftliche DiensteWD 8 – 3000 - 104/2009... sich die Rechte der Veröffentlichung und Verbreitung vor...</span></span></span>

Private investigators have consequently preserved much of the accessible history. Without the GEP dataset, numerous Baden-Württemberg reports would survive only as isolated press stories, personal memories or unpublished correspondence. The archive gives them dates, locations and investigative context, while its open release allows readers to look beyond selective accounts of the most dramatic cases.

Its limits are equally central to understanding the state’s history. The database is neither an official register nor a complete record of public experience. It is a curated collection of voluntary reports received and assessed by one organisation. That makes it a strong source for studying documented reporting and investigation, but a weak foundation for claims about how often unexplained objects actually appeared over Baden-Württemberg.

The fairest conclusion is that the GEP archive is indispensable but not comprehensive. It preserves the clearest continuous evidence trail available, helps distinguish ordinary misidentifications from genuinely unresolved files and exposes how frequently cases fail through missing information. Its greatest value is not that it proves an extraordinary phenomenon. It shows, in unusually concrete detail, what people reported, what investigators could test and where the surviving evidence stops.
