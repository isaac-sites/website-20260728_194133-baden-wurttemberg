## Introduction

The Flensburg meteor of 12 September 2019 is one of [Schleswig-Holstein]({{ 'what-is-really-seen-over-schleswig/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of an unusual object in the sky being identified through converging evidence. Hundreds of people saw a bright daylight fireball; cameras recorded its passage; researchers reconstructed its atmospheric path and earlier orbit; and, the next day, a freshly fallen meteorite was found in Flensburg. Laboratory analysis then confirmed that the stone was an exceptionally rare carbon-rich meteorite rather than terrestrial debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

That chain makes the incident valuable to the state’s wider UFO history. Witness testimony alone established that something dramatic had crossed the sky, but it did not establish what the object was. The firm explanation emerged only after independent recordings, trajectory calculations, satellite measurements and physical material were brought together. Flensburg therefore provides a useful standard against which weaker cases—especially single photographs, distant lights and unsupported recollections—can be judged.

## The fireball of 12 September 2019

At 12:49:48 UTC, or 14:49:48 local summer time, a large meteoroid entered the atmosphere over northern Europe. The resulting fireball was visible in daylight from parts of Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium and the United Kingdom. Reports from northern Germany also described a loud bang, consistent with shock waves produced by a fast object and its fragmentation high in the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

A daylight event of this kind can easily produce uncertainty in its first minutes. Observers see a brilliant object, often for only a few seconds, without reliable clues to its height, distance or true speed. Its apparent direction can differ substantially between towns, while cloud, perspective and the delay before any sound arrives can make accounts seem contradictory. Some early impressions reportedly placed a possible fall towards the North Sea; later calculations instead indicated that surviving pieces could have reached the German-Danish border region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flensburg_%28Meteorit%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Flensburg (Meteorit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flensburg (Meteorit</span></span></span>

The event was not merely a distant point of light. Researchers concluded that the original body was probably around two to three metres across, with an estimated pre-atmospheric mass of roughly 10,000 to 20,000 kilograms. It entered at about 19 kilometres per second and underwent severe fragmentation between approximately 46 and 37 kilometres altitude. These figures are model-based estimates rather than direct measurements of an intact asteroid, but they explain why a sizeable incoming body could leave only a tiny amount of recoverable material on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span></span></span>

The overwhelming majority of the mass was destroyed, dispersed as fine material or reduced to fragments too small to locate. Only one confirmed stone, weighing 24.5 grams, is known. That contrast—many tonnes before entry, one small recovered piece afterwards—is striking, but it is not evidence that large fragments disappeared mysteriously. The trajectory study found that the object was structurally weak and broke apart repeatedly under atmospheric stress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

## Camera reconstruction and orbital analysis

The camera record was unusually useful, although not perfect. The fireball was captured by a dedicated AllSky6 meteor camera at Herford and by three incidental recordings, including vehicle and security-camera footage from Germany and the Netherlands. Researchers compared the object’s position against the background scene in each recording, corrected for camera geometry and timing, and combined the different viewing lines to reconstruct a three-dimensional atmospheric track.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite...January 6, 2021 — by J Borovička · 2021 · Cited by 34 — The C1-ungroupe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

This method is far stronger than interpreting a single video. One camera can show angular movement across an image but cannot, by itself, reliably establish distance. Recordings from separated locations provide intersecting lines of sight, allowing investigators to estimate the object’s altitude, direction and speed. Even ordinary dashcam or security footage can become scientifically valuable when the camera position, lens characteristics, orientation and timing can be recovered.

The reconstruction indicated that the meteoroid approached from the south and travelled northwards before its final disruption. By calculating its velocity before atmospheric braking and accounting for the movement of Earth, the team also worked backwards to estimate its orbit around the Sun. The most likely source region was near the 5:2 orbital resonance with Jupiter, at roughly 2.82 astronomical units from the Sun, in the outer part of the main asteroid belt. A resonance is a region where Jupiter’s repeated gravitational influence can gradually alter an asteroid’s orbit and help send fragments towards the inner Solar System.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span></span></span>

Space-based sensors supplied an additional line of evidence. United States government instruments detected the atmospheric explosion and provided an independent estimate of its energy and velocity vector. The camera reconstruction broadly agreed with the reported direction, although the scientific study found that the satellite-derived speed was almost one kilometre per second too low. This discrepancy is important: independent instruments can reinforce an identification without producing identical numbers, and apparent precision should not be confused with perfect accuracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span></span></span>

There were also limits. The fireball occurred during the day, parts of the region were cloudy, and the available camera geometry was less complete than that achieved by some purpose-built fireball networks. The researchers could determine the bright atmospheric path and pre-impact orbit, but predicting exactly where every small surviving fragment landed remained much harder. Once a meteorite becomes too slow and cool to glow, its “dark flight” is affected by winds, shape, mass and drag. Small uncertainties can shift a predicted landing point by hundreds of metres or more.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04397" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The Flensburg stone was therefore not found because scientists had already calculated a precise search location. It was discovered by chance. This weakens any claim that the camera reconstruction independently predicted the exact recovery spot, but it does not seriously weaken the link between the stone and the fireball. The discovery occurred one day after the event, within the relevant region, and the specimen showed the physical characteristics of an exceptionally fresh fall.

## Recovery and classification of the meteorite

On 13 September, a resident found an unusual dark stone on the lawn of his garden in the Flensburg district of Weiche. It weighed 24.5 grams and measured roughly 3.7 by 3.5 centimetres. Its black fusion crust—the thin melted surface produced during high-speed atmospheric passage—appeared fresh, with no significant terrestrial weathering. Specialists connected with the European Fireball Network recognised it as a probable meteorite and sent it to the Institute of Planetology at the University of Münster for detailed examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

Several facts made an accidental association with an old, unrelated meteorite highly improbable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it appeared immediately after a widely observed local fireball;
* it was found within the reconstructed fall region;
* its surface showed a fresh fusion crust;
* it received a weathering grade of W0, meaning no visible terrestrial alteration;
* laboratory tests confirmed a composition consistent with fragile extraterrestrial material rather than a common local rock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[LPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

</div>

The Meteoritical Society formally approved the specimen under the name **Flensburg** in February 2020. It was classified as an ungrouped C1 carbonaceous chondrite. “Carbonaceous” refers to a broad family of primitive meteorites rich in volatile elements and minerals altered by water. The “1” indicates extremely extensive aqueous alteration: much of the original rock was transformed by liquid water on its parent asteroid. “Ungrouped” means that its mineral and chemical characteristics do not fit neatly into any established meteorite group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

Classification was not based on appearance alone. Researchers used scanning electron microscopy, mineral chemistry, oxygen-isotope measurements and other laboratory techniques. They identified abundant water-bearing sheet silicates and carbonates, along with a mixture of rock fragments recording alteration, impacts and reassembly on the parent body. An international consortium involving dozens of researchers and institutions subsequently studied the tiny specimen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uni-muenster.de/news/view.php?cmdid=11499" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uni-muenster.de">[University of Münster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uni-muenster.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span></span></span>

The laboratory work produced a result of wider scientific importance. Dating of microscopic carbonate grains indicated that liquid-water alteration occurred only about three million years after the first known solids formed in the Solar System. The carbonates were more than a million years older than comparable material in several other carbonaceous meteorites, making Flensburg evidence for very early liquid water inside a small planetary body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uni-muenster.de/news/view.php?cmdid=11499" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uni-muenster.de">[University of Münster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uni-muenster.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span></span></span>

That finding should be expressed carefully. The stone does not prove that its particular parent asteroid supplied Earth’s oceans. It demonstrates that small, carbon-rich bodies containing water-altered minerals existed extremely early, supporting the broader possibility that similar asteroids contributed water and volatile material to the growing Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uni-muenster.de/news/view.php?cmdid=11499" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uni-muenster.de">[University of Münster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uni-muenster.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Münster Oldest Carbonates in the Solar System</span></span></span>

## Why the identification is unusually strong

The Flensburg case did not rest on one supposedly decisive clue. Its strength came from several partly independent forms of evidence that agreed on the same ordinary astronomical explanation.

**Witness evidence established the event.** Hundreds of observations showed that a bright, fast-moving phenomenon crossed a wide area at a specific time. Witnesses could not accurately determine its orbit or composition, but their reports confirmed the scale and timing of the occurrence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[LPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

**Videos fixed the geometry.** Recordings from separate locations allowed investigators to reconstruct the atmospheric path rather than relying on perceived direction or memory. The footage also documented fragmentation, helping to explain the loud reports and the small amount of material recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite...January 6, 2021 — by J Borovička · 2021 · Cited by 34 — The C1-ungroupe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

**Satellite data supplied an independent physical measurement.** Government sensors detected the event’s energy and direction. Although one reported velocity required correction, the data supported the conclusion that a metre-scale natural body had entered and exploded in the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02177" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Trajectory and orbit of the unique carbonaceous meteorite Flensburg</span></span></span>

**The recovered stone could be tested.** Its fresh crust, low weathering, mineralogy, isotopic composition and rare classification established that it was extraterrestrial. The timing and location tied it convincingly to the fireball.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lpi.usra.edu/meteor/metbull.php?code=71098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lpi.usra.edu">[usra.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lpi.usra.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meteoritical Bulletin: Entry for Flensburg5 Feb 2020 — Confirmed fall: 2019 Sept 12 Classification: Carbonaceous chondrite (C1, ungrou...</span></span></span>

**Later research strengthened rather than overturned the explanation.** The trajectory paper refined the object’s path and probable asteroid-belt origin, while laboratory studies revealed the meteorite’s water-altered history. Differences between individual measurements were examined openly rather than concealed, and none created a credible alternative to the meteor explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016703720306463" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

## The standard it sets for Schleswig-Holstein UFO cases

Flensburg matters to Schleswig-Holstein’s aerial-mystery history precisely because it became identified. A phenomenon may initially be a UFO in the literal sense—an object that observers cannot identify—without remaining unexplained after investigation. The witnesses were not necessarily mistaken about seeing something extraordinary. They accurately reported a brilliant object and explosive sounds. What they could not determine from the ground was its distance, composition and origin.

The case also shows why a dramatic appearance is not a measure of evidential mystery. A daylight fireball visible across several countries, accompanied by detonations and produced by a multi-tonne body, is more visually spectacular than most reported UFOs. Yet it is also more securely explained because it left measurable traces at every stage.

By contrast, many regional UFO reports lack one or more of the elements that made Flensburg solvable. A blurred photograph may have no known camera direction. A light over the Baltic may have no second viewpoint. A witness may report apparent speed without knowing the object’s distance. Radar data may be unavailable, and no physical material may be recovered. In those circumstances, “unresolved” usually describes an evidence shortage rather than positive evidence for an exotic craft.

Flensburg therefore offers a practical hierarchy for judging claims:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Contemporaneous records** are generally stronger than memories collected years later.
2. **Several viewpoints** are more useful than repeated copies of one image.
3. **Calibrated data** are stronger than visual estimates of speed or altitude.
4. **Independent sensor types** can expose errors while still supporting the overall explanation.
5. **Recoverable physical evidence** permits tests that photographs and testimony cannot.
6. **Published methods and uncertainties** make an identification more credible, not less.

</div>

No single future sighting needs to reproduce every feature of the Flensburg investigation before it can be explained. Aircraft-tracking records, astronomical calculations or a clear sequence of photographs may be sufficient in simpler cases. But extraordinary interpretations should not be treated as strongly evidenced when they rest on far less information than was required to reconstruct this demonstrably natural event.

The final assessment is therefore unusually firm. The Flensburg object was a carbon-rich meteoroid from the asteroid belt that fragmented over northern Europe and deposited at least one small meteorite in Schleswig-Holstein. Some details—such as the number and final locations of unrecovered fragments—remain unknown. Those gaps concern the fine reconstruction of a well-identified meteorite fall, not the basic nature of the object.
