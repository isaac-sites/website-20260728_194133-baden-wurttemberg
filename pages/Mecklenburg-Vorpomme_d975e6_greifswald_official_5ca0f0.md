## Introduction

The official record of the [Greifswald lights]({{ 'greifswald-case/' | relative_url }}) is incomplete in a very specific sense. Later military correspondence, testimony from former service personnel and research into the Baltic training area strongly support the view that the lights seen on 24 August 1990 were parachute-supported illumination or target flares. What has never appeared publicly, however, is a complete operational file naming the unit, aircraft or ship, ammunition type, release times and exact firing positions for every stage of the display.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Mecklenburg-Vorpomme_d975e6_greifswald_official_5ca0f0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Records" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters. The flare explanation does not depend on proving that extraterrestrial craft were impossible; it succeeds because it fits the filmed behaviour, the known function of the training zone and accounts from people familiar with the equipment. Yet the missing mission-level paperwork prevents the case from ending with the documentary neatness many readers expect. Greifswald is therefore best described as persuasively explained but not completely reconstructed from surviving public records.

## The 1994 official response

The closest thing to an early official identification came four years after the sighting. According to investigator Werner Walter’s published account, Rudolf Henke of the German sceptics’ organisation GWUP persuaded an officer in the German Ministry of Defence’s air-force press office to watch an ARD television programme broadcast on 24 October 1994. The following day, Lieutenant Colonel Booth reportedly identified the objects shown in the programme as parachute-supported signal bombs, known in western military terminology as “fire pots”, which could remain alight for several minutes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gwup.org/skeptiker-artikel/pseudo-parawissenschaften/das-ufo-phaenomen-von-greifswald-ein-deutscher-klassiker/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwup.org">[GWUP e. V.]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwup.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald - Ein deutscher Klassiker &#124; Pseudo- und Parawissenschaften &#124; GWUP e. V...</span></span></span>

This response was important because it came from a serving military press officer rather than solely from civilian UFO investigators. It established that the filmed objects looked familiar to someone professionally connected with military aviation. It also corrected a persistent misunderstanding: the proposed explanation was not ordinary tracer ammunition, which travels rapidly along a ballistic path, but slower-burning pyrotechnic devices suspended beneath parachutes.

Even so, the 1994 intervention was an identification from television footage, not a disclosed operations report for 24 August 1990. The surviving published account does not reproduce a sortie log, weapons register, exercise order or radar plot bearing Booth’s conclusion. It therefore has evidential value as expert recognition, but it cannot by itself prove which unit released the lights or at what coordinates.

A separate strand of correspondence reportedly came from the Swedish embassy in 1994 and was later publicised by Walter as support for identifying the lights as military infrared decoys. The publicly accessible summaries confirm that such a letter was cited, but they do not provide a complete chain of operational documentation linking Swedish forces to the Greifswald event. It is therefore safer to treat the embassy material as technical corroboration of the type of phenomenon rather than proof that Sweden conducted the specific activity witnessed that evening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.gwup.net/2015/08/24/die-greifswald-ufos-fliegen-immer-noch-neue-videos-zu-einem-alten-phanomen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.gwup.net">[GWUP Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.gwup.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP BlogDie „Greifswald-Ufos“ fliegen immer noch – neue Videos zu einem alten Phänomen &#124; gwup &#124; die skeptiker...</span></span></span>

## Conflicting and partial documentation

The documentation problem is not simply that “the military denied everything”. Different enquiries produced answers at different levels, and those answers were not always addressing the same question.

In 2012, the German UFO organisation DEGUFO stated that neither the East German air-force high command nor records consulted at the Federal Archives’ Military Archive confirmed an exercise in the area at the stated time. It argued that the [flare theory]({{ 'flare-theory-899bec/' | relative_url }}) had not been demonstrated and maintained that no responsible military party had been identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openpr.de/news/658020/Greifswald-UFO-Raetsel-seit-22-Jahren-ungeloest.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openpr.de">[openPR.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openpr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">open PR.de Greifswald UFO-Rätsel seit 22 Jahren ungelöst</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greifswald UFO-Rätsel seit 22 Jahren ungelöst...</span></span></span>

That claim appears strong until the limits of a negative archive search are considered. A record showing no exercise by a named East German formation would not exclude activity by Soviet, Polish or Czechoslovak forces in an international Warsaw Pact training zone. Nor would the absence of a document under one unit’s files prove that no flare release occurred from a ship, aircraft or command structure whose papers were held elsewhere.

The geography makes this particularly important. Former East German military personnel identified the waters east of Rügen and north of Usedom as Air Firing Zone II, a Warsaw Pact range used for aerial weapons training. Walter’s account says that former East German air-force spokesman Franz-Lorenz Lill described both aircraft-dropped illumination devices and ship-launched luminous targets being used there. A later regional report on military aviation history likewise described aircraft from East Germany, Czechoslovakia and the Soviet 16th Air Army firing air-to-air missiles at bright targets dropped at high altitude in the same zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gwup.org/skeptiker-artikel/pseudo-parawissenschaften/das-ufo-phaenomen-von-greifswald-ein-deutscher-klassiker/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwup.org">[GWUP e. V.]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwup.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald - Ein deutscher Klassiker &#124; Pseudo- und Parawissenschaften &#124; GWUP e. V...</span></span></span>

Further research published in 2012 proposed a more precise scenario: Czechoslovak aircraft releasing SAB-type illumination bombs, each carrying seven pyrotechnic flares, at an altitude of about ten kilometres during an exercise in Air Firing Zone II. The repeated seven-light groupings visible in the Greifswald films are one reason this reconstruction attracted attention. Its author presented it as a provisional conclusion, however, and critics continued to ask which exact unit flew the mission and where the confirming order or flight log could be inspected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.gwup.net/2012/11/25/greifswald-ufo-case-closed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.gwup.net">[GWUP Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.gwup.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Blog Greifswald-Ufo: Case closed &#124; gwup &#124; die skeptiker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP BlogGreifswald-Ufo: Case closed &#124; gwup &#124; die skeptiker...</span></span></span>

Witness testimony fills some of the gaps but also complicates the record. Several local sailors and former military personnel told Walter that they recognised the objects as parachute-supported targets or illumination bombs. One former National People’s Army officer, Lueder Stock, reportedly said he saw small rockets launch the lights from a warship and had previously participated in exercises using similar targets. These accounts are highly relevant because they describe mechanisms visible in the footage: gradual descent, changing relative positions, brief flashes and repeated formations. Yet they are retrospective statements collected mainly after renewed media coverage in September 1994. They are not substitutes for contemporary ship logs or weapons records, and Stock’s attribution of the incoming fire to the Polish direction does not definitively identify the operator.

## Why the archive trail is broken

The timing of the incident helps explain why a clean “Greifswald file” may never have existed in a single archive. The sighting occurred only weeks before German reunification, while the East German armed forces were being dismantled and Warsaw Pact structures were entering rapid decline.

The Federal Archives’ own description of the East German military archive shows how disruptive this transition was. From late 1990, records from the defence ministry, navy, air forces, air defence and border troops were gradually transferred into an interim military archive. Many arrived unordered and uncatalogued. The archive notes that time pressure during the dissolution of military institutions often made orderly, fully documented transfers impossible, and that some material remained only provisionally arranged for years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archivportal-d.de/item/ETXTS6LAETQ446AYSMFBP57R4Y3FOIIE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivportal-d.de">[Archivportal-D]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivportal-d.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Detailseite - Archivportal-D...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that a Greifswald operations file was destroyed. It does show why “the archive contains no matching exercise record” is not equivalent to “no exercise occurred”. Records might have been filed by participating unit rather than location, retained in another Warsaw Pact country, never transferred, recorded only in routine logs that were not preserved, or dispersed among aviation, naval and range-control collections.

The multinational character of Air Firing Zone II creates a further obstacle. If Czechoslovak aircraft used Soviet-designed flares in a range controlled or monitored through East German and Soviet structures, a conclusive reconstruction could require records from several countries. German archives may contain range closures or local support arrangements without holding the visiting unit’s weapons log. Polish or Czech archives might preserve flight orders without the German coastal observations that make their significance apparent.

There is also disagreement about whether the relevant exercises normally continued after dark. One former serviceman recalled an Antonov An-24 flying range-safety patrols from Garz during comparable exercises, but believed those flights had taken place in daylight. He accepted that night activity might have been supported from another airfield, while noting that his own station had not remained on night alert. His recollection supports the existence of the training system but illustrates why memories cannot settle the precise schedule of 24 August 1990.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.gwup.net/2012/11/25/greifswald-ufo-case-closed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.gwup.net">[GWUP Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.gwup.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Blog Greifswald-Ufo: Case closed &#124; gwup &#124; die skeptiker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP BlogGreifswald-Ufo: Case closed &#124; gwup &#124; die skeptiker...</span></span></span>

## What a conclusive record would require

A genuinely conclusive public file would need to join together several kinds of evidence that currently survive only as fragments. At minimum, it would identify:

* the exercise authority and participating national forces;
* the aircraft, ship or coastal launcher that released each formation;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the exact pyrotechnic device and the number of burning elements it carried;
* take-off, launch, release and recovery times;
* the coordinates and altitude of each release;
* range-closure or maritime safety notices;
* meteorological data showing the direction and speed of drift;
* ammunition expenditure records;
* radar, radio or air-traffic logs;
* and contemporary reports of missile firing or brief explosions near the targets.

</div>

Such a file would allow investigators to compare recorded release times with the separate videos, calculate whether the lights should have drifted towards Peenemünde, and test whether successive flare packages could account for the reported overall duration. It would also resolve the persistent dispute over whether the responsible platform was a ship, an East German aircraft, a Czechoslovak formation or another Warsaw Pact unit.

No complete public set of documents meeting that standard has emerged. The 1994 military recognition, the identification of the range, the known use of seven-element pyrotechnic devices and the testimony of experienced local observers form a persuasive cumulative case. They do not form an unbroken bureaucratic chain from an exercise order to an ammunition return.

## Does the missing file keep the case unresolved?

The missing operational record leaves a documentary question open, but it does not return every possible explanation to equal standing. An unexplained light is not automatically evidence of an unknown craft, and a missing log is not positive evidence of concealment.

The flare interpretation accounts for several otherwise unusual features at once: the slow descent, the silence at long range, the loose but recognisable formations, the fading sequence, the apparent smoke or flicker reported in some footage, and the brief flashes near the lights. It also places the event inside a documented military training area where comparable luminous targets were routinely used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gwup.org/skeptiker-artikel/pseudo-parawissenschaften/das-ufo-phaenomen-von-greifswald-ein-deutscher-klassiker/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwup.org">[gwup.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwup.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GWUP e. V.Das UFO-Phänomen von Greifswald - Ein deutscher Klassiker &#124; Pseudo- und Parawissenschaften &#124; GWUP e. V...</span></span></span>

The doubts are narrower. Investigators have not publicly demonstrated the exact mission, and competing reconstructions have named different possible operators. Some claims about specific aircraft, ammunition and national forces depend on former personnel or specialist researchers rather than released primary records. DEGUFO’s objection that the responsible military party had not been conclusively identified therefore remains fair, even though its broader rejection of the flare explanation is less persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openpr.de/news/658020/Greifswald-UFO-Raetsel-seit-22-Jahren-ungeloest.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openpr.de">[openPR.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openpr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">open PR.de Greifswald UFO-Rätsel seit 22 Jahren ungelöst</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greifswald UFO-Rätsel seit 22 Jahren ungelöst...</span></span></span>

For [Mecklenburg-Vorpommern]({{ 'mecklenburg-vorpommern/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history, the Greifswald record is most useful as a lesson in evidential grading. The event itself was real and well filmed. The conventional explanation is strong. The official documentation is incomplete. Those three statements can coexist without contradiction.

The case should therefore not be labelled wholly unexplained, but neither should the missing file be quietly replaced by a certainty the records do not support. “Probably military flares, with the precise operation still undocumented in public” is less dramatic than either an alien encounter or a perfectly closed case. It is also the assessment that best fits the surviving evidence.
