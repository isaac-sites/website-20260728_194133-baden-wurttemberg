## Introduction

[Saxony-Anhalt]({{ 'why-saxony-anhalts-ufo-mysteries-usually/' | relative_url }})’s clearest structured record of modern UFO reporting is not a government census but a private case dataset published by the Society for the Study of the UFO Phenomenon, known as the GEP. Filtering its 1972–2023 release for Saxony-Anhalt produces 125 records. Most point towards familiar causes: lanterns and small balloons, aircraft, satellites, bright planets, camera artefacts and other ordinary objects. Only three entries are explicitly classified as having no identification, while several others remain blank or lack enough information for a conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2023 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Saxony-Anhalt_5678a0_modern_ufo_case_data_ce46ac-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Case Data" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The dataset therefore does not show that unexplained craft are common over Saxony-Anhalt. What it does show—more usefully—is how the objects that generate UFO reports change over time, how investigation reduces apparent mystery, and why “[unresolved]({{ 'unresolved-5ef740/' | relative_url }})” must not be confused with “extraordinary”. It is a record of reports received and processed by one organisation, not a measurement of everything seen in the state.

## How the case dataset was assembled

The GEP has received and investigated reports from members of the public since 1972. Its published CSV dataset contains 5,577 records from Germany and elsewhere, including the sighting date and place, time, method of first contact, a free-text account, descriptive classifications and any identification reached by investigators. Personal details have been removed for privacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2023 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>

This structure makes the collection more informative than a simple list of “UFO sightings”. A reader can distinguish between the event originally described, the information available to the investigator and the eventual assessment. That matters because a bright stationary light may begin as an unidentified object but end as [Venus]({{ 'venus/' | relative_url }}); a procession of evenly spaced lights may be traced to [Starlink]({{ 'starlink-f2efa7/' | relative_url }}) satellites; and a glowing object recorded by a camera may turn out to be an insect, reflection or lens effect.

GEP investigators describe using structured witness information, image examination, astronomical and aviation checks, and comparisons with known objects and perceptual effects. The organisation also applies established descriptive systems associated with astronomer J. Allen Hynek and investigator Allan Hendry, supplemented by a verification scale developed by Rudolf Henke.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380530617_UAP_Research_in_Germany_Single_Case_Studies_Data_Management_Understanding_of_Strangeness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) UAP Research in Germany: Single Case Studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) UAP Research in Germany: Single Case Studies</span></span></span>

The verification codes are important because not every ordinary explanation has the same evidential strength:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **V1** indicates a close match in time and geographical position with a known object or event.
* **V2** means the reported characteristics correspond well with a known phenomenon, allowing for normal perceptual error.
* **V3** is a broader conclusion in which the account resembles more than one possible conventional stimulus.

</div>

Among the Saxony-Anhalt records, 25 carry a V1 code, 38 carry V2 and six carry V3. Fifty-six have no verification code, often because the case is unfinished, unclassified, too poorly documented or not assigned a specific conventional explanation in the published fields. The absence of a code is therefore not itself evidence of an anomalous object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2023 &#124; Zenodo...</span></span></span>

The dates also require care. Although the release is labelled 1972–2023, the Saxony-Anhalt subset includes retrospective accounts said to have occurred in 1968 and 1969. This reflects the difference between when an event allegedly happened and when information about it entered the archive. A childhood memory reported decades later does not have the same evidential value as a promptly submitted observation with photographs, exact timing and a known viewing direction.

## Which patterns appear most often

The strongest pattern is not a single dominant explanation but a succession of technologies and habits that created different kinds of reports at different times.

### Lanterns and balloons dominate many earlier cases

Model hot-air balloons and sky lanterns form the largest clearly identified group in the Saxony-Anhalt subset. Twenty records are assigned to the combined category covering model balloons and sky lanterns, while another twelve involve foil balloons, research or weather balloons, illuminated balloons or similar lighter-than-air objects. Together, these account for more than a quarter of all 125 state records.

The concentration around 2008 is especially revealing. Eleven Saxony-Anhalt cases in the dataset carry that year, many involving isolated or multiple orange lights subsequently identified as model hot-air balloons or lanterns. Such objects can drift silently, appear to hold formation and disappear as their flames fail or they enter cloud. Their apparent speed and spacing are difficult to judge at night because [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses-9fc6b4/' | relative_url }}) usually have no reliable estimate of distance.

One 13 July 2008 report from Eckartsberga was assigned a mixed explanation involving both a fireball and a sky lantern. That classification is a reminder that witnesses may combine separate stimuli into one narrative. A brief meteor and a slower orange light seen within the same period can be remembered as different phases of a single object rather than two unrelated events.

### Aircraft remain surprisingly difficult to recognise

Ten records are grouped with aircraft, helicopters or condensation trails. Some concern landing lights, which can look nearly stationary when an aircraft is approaching directly towards an observer. Others involve illuminated trails whose apparent shape changes with sunlight, cloud and perspective.

This is not necessarily a sign of careless observation. At night, an aircraft’s distance and direction are often ambiguous until its navigation lights, engine noise or lateral movement become obvious. A bright head-on landing lamp may appear to hover, grow brighter and then “turn” abruptly when the aircraft changes course. Atmospheric haze can hide the airframe and leave only a light that seems detached from any conventional vehicle.

The dataset consequently shows why descriptions such as “silent”, “stationary” or “moving strangely” are not decisive by themselves. Sound may not reach the witness immediately, and movement towards or away from an observer produces little visible motion against the background sky. ### Starlink created a recognisable new report type

Satellite-related explanations account for ten Saxony-Anhalt records, including six specifically identified as SpaceX Starlink satellites. These cases become prominent only in the recent portion of the archive, reflecting a genuine change in the night sky rather than a sudden change in witness reliability.

A Starlink “train” consists of newly deployed satellites travelling along a similar orbit before they spread out. Shortly after launch, they can appear as a precise chain of white lights crossing the sky and vanishing one after another as they enter Earth’s shadow. Space specialists have described the formation as a bright “string of pearls”, a pattern striking enough to trigger reports wherever a launch pass is visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int/proceedings/sdc8/paper/208/SDC8-paper208.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int">[ESA Proceedings Database]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Proceedings Database BRINGING POLICY COHERENCE TO SATELLITE</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proceedings Database BRINGING POLICY COHERENCE TO SATELLITE</span></span></span>

The Saxony-Anhalt entries from December 2023 illustrate the distinction between a resolved and an unfinished case. A report near Zeitz described roughly 12 to 15 lights travelling from west to east and disappearing sequentially after about 30 seconds; investigators identified them as Starlink. A report from Haldensleben on the same date described a flying chain of lights but had no published identification in the 2023 release. The similarity makes Starlink a plausible hypothesis for the second case, but the blank database field should not be silently converted into a formal solution.

That difference is central to responsible use of the data. Pattern recognition can suggest an explanation, but a defensible identification requires the time, direction and satellite pass to be checked for the particular location.

### Planets produce persistent lights and apparent movement

Seven records are grouped with planets, stars or the Moon. Venus alone appears as the stated identification in three Saxony-Anhalt cases, including a December 2023 report from Zörbig.

Bright planets are frequent sources of UFO reports because they can appear unexpectedly intense near the horizon. Thin cloud, atmospheric turbulence and involuntary eye movements can make a point of light seem to pulse, change colour or move slightly. Without foreground reference points, an observer may also interpret a planet as a nearby object rather than a distant celestial body. Astronomy explainers identify bright stars and planets—especially when low in the sky—as recurring causes of apparently hovering or erratically moving lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earthsky.org/space/if-its-not-a-ufo-what-is-it/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthsky.org">[EarthSky]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthsky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Earth Sky Is that a UFO?! There&#x27;s probably an explanation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Earth Sky Is that a UFO?! There&#x27;s probably an explanation</span></span></span>

These cases are useful because they show that duration does not automatically strengthen an extraordinary interpretation. A light remaining in roughly the same position for ten minutes or two hours may be more consistent with an astronomical body than with a nearby craft. The decisive questions are its compass direction, elevation and whether its apparent position matches the sky at the reported time.

### Cameras add objects the witness never saw

Eight records are grouped with insects, birds, particles, water drops or similar material close to a camera. Three others are identified as lens reflections. These reports belong to a different evidential category from direct visual observations: the “object” is often discovered only when a photograph or wildlife-camera recording is reviewed later.

A nearby insect illuminated by infrared light can appear as a large, blurred and fast-moving form. A spider or strand of web close to a security camera may glow brightly while the background remains dark. Lens reflections can create coloured discs or duplicate lights that move in relation to the camera rather than the sky. Such effects can look structured in a single frame precisely because the camera has rendered them in a way the naked eye would not.

The dataset therefore supports an important distinction between photographs of a witnessed phenomenon and unexplained marks found afterwards. A picture can add evidence when it records the same event described independently by the observer. It is much weaker when the image itself is the only reason anyone believes an aerial event occurred.

## What the unresolved entries mean

The Saxony-Anhalt subset contains three records explicitly assigned to the identification group “no identification”. A further nine are labelled as having insufficient data. Thirty-two have no entry in the identification field at all.

These categories should not be combined into one dramatic total of 44 “unexplained UFOs”. They describe different situations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **No identification** means investigators did not reach a satisfactory solution from the available case.
* **Insufficient data** means the evidence was too limited to test explanations properly.
* **A blank field** may indicate an open case, incomplete processing, missing legacy information or no published determination in that version of the dataset.

</div>

The GEP’s current online statistics visibly distinguish open records from completed ones, reinforcing the point that an unfilled classification can be an administrative status rather than a scientific conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/GEP_UAP_Statistik.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

Some blank Saxony-Anhalt cases are intriguing on the page. A 1997 account from Zeitz describes a silent disc with a dome hovering near a road. A 2021 report from the Leuna area describes a dark, saucer-shaped object emerging from cloud. Another account alleges a luminous figure near Halberstadt, while a Bismark record concerns an abduction experience rather than a straightforward aerial observation.

Yet vividness does not supply missing measurements. These accounts generally lack independent sensor confirmation, reliable range estimates, contemporaneous photographs or enough environmental data to reconstruct the event. The extraordinary-sounding cases are therefore not necessarily the strongest cases. They are often the ones least capable of being checked.

An unresolved label means only that the submitted material did not support a confident identification. NASA’s independent UAP work has made the same broader methodological point: eyewitness reports can prompt investigation, but poor calibration, missing metadata and inconsistent observations severely restrict what can be concluded scientifically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science UAP FAQs</span></span></span>

## Why reports cannot measure prevalence

The 125 Saxony-Anhalt records are not a complete census of sightings in the state. They are a self-selected collection of experiences that reached a particular private organisation and were retained in its database.

There is no defined population from which the reports were randomly sampled. Residents who saw nothing had no reason to contact the GEP. Many people who did see something unusual may have told friends, posted online, contacted police or simply forgotten it. Others may not have known the organisation existed. Statistical guidance is clear that population estimates require a sampling design in which members of the target population have a known chance of selection; self-selected, non-probability data cannot by itself establish prevalence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gesis.org/fileadmin/admin/Dateikatalog/pdf/guidelines/non_probability_surveys_rohr_felderer_silber_daikeler_rossmann_schroeder_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gesis.org">[GESIS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gesis.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When are non-probability surveys fit for my purpose?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When are non-probability surveys fit for my purpose?</span></span></span>

Raw case counts are also shaped by changing access to reporting. The expansion of the internet, camera phones, social media and online submission forms makes recent reporting easier than it was during the 1970s or in East Germany. A rise from one decade to another could reflect awareness of the reporting service rather than an increase in unusual aerial activity.

Specific events can produce bursts without indicating a regional hotspot. A Starlink pass may generate several reports on one evening. The legal popularity of sky lanterns during a particular period can create a cluster during summer celebrations or New Year. A widely circulated news story can encourage people to submit old memories. These are changes in exposure and reporting behaviour, not necessarily changes in the underlying rate of genuinely anomalous phenomena.

Geographical comparisons are similarly hazardous. More entries from Halle or [Magdeburg]({{ 'magdeburg/' | relative_url }}) could result from their larger populations, denser internet use or greater visibility of the GEP, while darker rural districts may offer better sky conditions but fewer potential reporters. Without population adjustment, consistent reporting coverage and information about unreported observations, a map of cases is a map of submissions—not a map of UFO activity.

## What the records actually establish

The dataset’s most defensible contribution to Saxony-Anhalt’s UFO history is a picture of recurring misidentification and changing visual culture.

It shows a period in which glowing lanterns and small balloons produced many reports, followed by an era in which satellite trains became a recognisable source of linear light formations. It shows that planets and aircraft remain effective UFO stimuli despite being familiar objects, and that modern cameras create a growing class of apparent anomalies that were never directly observed. It also preserves a small residue of cases that cannot be resolved from the available information.

That residue should neither be dismissed nor exaggerated. Unresolved reports can identify weaknesses in reporting systems and show what information future investigators need: exact times, viewing directions, original image files, weather conditions, satellite predictions, aircraft data and independent observations. What they do not provide, on their own, is proof of an unknown technology or extraterrestrial origin.

For Saxony-Anhalt, the central lesson is therefore methodological. The modern records are strongest when used to study how people encounter and interpret unfamiliar lights. They are much weaker when treated as a count of mysterious objects physically present over the state. The archive documents 125 reported cases; it does not document 125 unexplained craft.
