## Introduction

Most ordinary UFO reports in [Mecklenburg-Vorpommern]({{ 'mecklenburg-vorpommern/' | relative_url }}) do not describe structured craft performing impossible manoeuvres. They describe lights: a glowing point that appears to hover, a fiery streak, a silent chain crossing the sky, or several lamps moving in formation. In many cases, those appearances match balloons, meteors, bright planets, satellites, aircraft or drones seen without reliable information about distance and scale.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Mecklenburg-Vorpomme_d975e6_common_ufo_explanati_51afb7-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Common Causes" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Two regional examples show why these explanations matter. A silent light reported near Moraas and Hagenow-Heide in July 2008 was plausibly identified as a lantern-like balloon, while a green, elongated glow east of Neubrandenburg on 31 October 2015 was assessed by police aviation personnel as a small meteor burning up in the atmosphere. More recently, a line of nine lights reported from Neubrandenburg in January 2024 matched a Starlink satellite train. These are not dishonest or foolish reports. They show how normal objects become genuinely difficult to identify when seen briefly, at night and without a fixed reference point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://katapult-mv.de/artikel/e-t-in-mv/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: katapult-mv.de">[katapult-mv.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">katapult-mv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span></span></span>

## Balloons and lantern-like lights

The 2008 reports from the former Ludwigslust district are a useful example of how a simple airborne object can acquire apparently mysterious properties. Witnesses around Moraas and Hagenow-Heide described an intense, single-coloured light that made no sound, did not blink and then disappeared. Those details seemed to exclude an ordinary aeroplane or helicopter. UFO investigator Werner Walter instead proposed one of the lantern-like balloons that had recently become popular at summer celebrations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://katapult-mv.de/artikel/e-t-in-mv/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: katapult-mv.de">[KATAPULT MV]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">katapult-mv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span></span></span>

Such objects are easily misread because the observer normally sees only the illuminated flame or envelope, not the fragile paper structure around it. A sky lantern is effectively a miniature hot-air balloon: a flame heats the air inside a lightweight paper covering, producing enough lift for the object to drift with the wind. Its course cannot be controlled, and its apparent movement depends on wind direction, viewing angle and distance. Seen head-on, it may look stationary; seen through thin cloud, it can brighten, fade or vanish abruptly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himmelslaterne" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HimmelslaterneDer Auftrieb wird durch Erwärmung der im Ballonkörper enthaltenen Luft mittels einer eigenen Feuerquelle erzeugt. Several common features make balloons and lanterns strong candidates in reports of slow, silent lights:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A steady orange, red or yellow glow** is more consistent with a flame than with standard aircraft lighting.
* **No audible engine** is unsurprising when the object is small, distant and wind-borne.
* **Slow drifting or apparent hovering** can result when it moves towards or away from the witness rather than across the field of view.
* **Several lights following similar paths** may reflect multiple lanterns released from the same wedding, festival or private gathering.
* **Sudden disappearance** can occur when the fuel is exhausted, the lantern turns, cloud intervenes or the object passes behind trees.

</div>

The main weakness in the Ludwigslust explanation is evidential rather than physical. No recovered lantern, launch record or identified celebration appears in the surviving public account. The proposed identification nevertheless fits the reported colour, silence, season and movement better than an unknown powered craft. The case should therefore be regarded as plausibly explained, not conclusively proved.

Balloons can create other misleading forms. A reflective helium balloon may shine white in sunlight after the ground below has entered dusk, while a group tethered together can rotate and repeatedly change shape. At greater distance, the human eye cannot reliably judge whether such a point is a small nearby object or a much larger object far away. This missing distance information is one of the most persistent problems in interpreting single-witness UFO reports.

## Meteors, planets and satellites

### The Neubrandenburg fireball

At about 7 pm on Halloween night in 2015, several people independently reported an unusual light east of Neubrandenburg. Accounts referred to a long, greenish or vertically extended glow, with some witnesses describing a bright green illumination of the sky and a possible bang. The police were contacted, and members of a police helicopter unit also confirmed seeing the phenomenon. Their assessment was that it was probably a small meteoroid burning up in the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://katapult-mv.de/artikel/e-t-in-mv/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: katapult-mv.de">[KATAPULT MV]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">katapult-mv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span></span></span>

The phrase “small meteorite” used in contemporary reporting is technically imprecise. A rock in space is a meteoroid; the visible atmospheric streak is a meteor; only material that survives to reach the ground is a meteorite. Bright examples are commonly called fireballs. NASA notes that meteors are produced when meteoroids enter the atmosphere at high speed, heating and creating a luminous trail, while almost all incoming material is vaporised before reaching the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Meteors and Meteorites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceMeteors and Meteorites - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

A meteor fits the Neubrandenburg description for several reasons. Fireballs can be green or blue-green because of the composition of the incoming material and excited atmospheric gases. Their light may illuminate thin cloud, making the phenomenon look broader than the object’s actual trail. Fragmentation can produce multiple bright points, and any delayed sound may arrive after the visible event because light reaches the observer much faster than sound.

This case also demonstrates why multiple witnesses do not automatically imply an extraordinary object. A bright meteor can be visible across a very large area, so people separated by many kilometres may independently report the same event. Confirmation by a helicopter crew strengthens the conclusion that a real light was present, but it does not establish that the source was an aircraft. The reported shape, brief duration and extended glow are more characteristic of an atmospheric entry than a hovering vehicle.

### Venus and other apparently motionless lights

Bright planets account for a quieter class of UFO report: a light that remains in roughly the same part of the sky, appears exceptionally brilliant and may seem to change colour or move slightly. Venus is the most frequent source because it is the brightest planet visible from Earth and, after the Sun and Moon, can be the brightest natural object in the sky. A planet close to the horizon is especially deceptive. Turbulent air bends its light unevenly, producing rapid changes in brightness and colour. Thin moving clouds can make the point seem to accelerate or drift in the opposite direction. The illusion becomes stronger when the observer watches from a moving car or looks between swaying trees. NASA’s guidance on identifying unusual aerial lights specifically notes that observers frequently ask about a brilliant light that turns out to be Venus and recommends checking the exact date, time and direction against planetarium software.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/identifying-ufos-and-uaps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Identifying UFOs and UAPs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Identifying UFOs and UAPs</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2013</span></span></span>

Planets can usually be tested without specialist equipment. A celestial object will remain in a predictable position relative to nearby stars and will shift only slowly as the sky turns. If the light is visible in the same place on successive evenings, does not cross the sky within minutes and matches an astronomical chart, the planetary explanation is substantially stronger than an aircraft or drone interpretation.

### Satellite trains over Neubrandenburg

Satellites have changed the appearance of the night sky more dramatically than most older UFO literature anticipated. A single low-orbit satellite normally looks like a star moving steadily and silently across the sky. It does not display the flashing red, green and white navigation lights associated with conventional aircraft, and it may fade abruptly as it enters Earth’s shadow. The International Space Station can be particularly bright because of its large reflective solar panels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Blue_dot/Spot_the_Space_Station_looking_at_you" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esa.int">[European Space Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esa.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">European Space Agency ESA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Space Agency ESA</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2014</span></span></span>

Freshly launched Starlink satellites produce a more striking pattern. Before dispersing into their operational orbits, a batch can appear as a straight procession of evenly spaced lights. In January 2024, a witness in Neubrandenburg reported nine luminous points in a row, disappearing one after another. Similar reports came from several parts of northern Germany. Contemporary coverage identified the formation as a Starlink train rather than a fleet of unknown craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bild.de/regional/niedersachsen/regional/merkwuerdige-lichterkette-am-nachthimmel-ufo-alarm-ueber-norddeutschland-86843792.bildMobile.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bild.de">[BILD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bild.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merkwürdige Lichterkette am Nachthimmel: UFO-Alarm über Norddeutschland &#124; Regional &#124; BILD.de...</span></span></span>

The order in which the lights vanish is an important clue. Satellites shine mainly by reflecting sunlight. As successive objects enter Earth’s shadow, they can fade one after another at almost the same region of sky. To an observer unfamiliar with orbital lighting, this can look like vehicles switching off their lamps in sequence.

Three distinctions are particularly useful:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A **meteor** normally crosses part of the sky in seconds and often leaves a streak.
* A **single satellite** moves smoothly for several minutes without sharp turns.
* A **recently launched satellite train** consists of numerous points following the same straight track at similar speed.

</div>

Satellites do not explain lights that hover at low altitude, reverse course or remain within a small area for a long period. But apparent changes of direction should be treated cautiously. Hand movement, loss of visual reference and the autokinetic effect — the tendency of a fixed light in darkness to seem to wander — can create motion that is not present in the object itself.

## Aircraft and drones at night

Aircraft remain among the most ordinary sources of unusual-light reports, particularly when they are approaching the observer. A plane flying almost directly towards a witness may show a bright landing light while appearing nearly motionless. Its sideways movement becomes obvious only when it turns or passes closer. Atmospheric conditions can suppress engine noise, especially when the aircraft is distant or the wind carries sound away from the observer.

Navigation lights can also create unfamiliar patterns. Depending on orientation, a witness may see only a white light, a red or green wingtip lamp, or an alternating strobe. When several aircraft follow the same approach route, their lights can appear to hover in a queue. This possibility is particularly relevant around Rostock Airport, military training areas and the state’s coastal flight corridors, although a convincing identification requires exact time, direction and flight-path data rather than proximity alone.

Drones complicate the picture because they can genuinely hover, change direction abruptly and operate at heights where sound is faint or intermittent. At night, the airframe may be invisible while only one or more LEDs can be seen. A drone turning in place can make different lamps appear and disappear, creating the impression that a luminous object has changed shape. Remote-controlled aircraft are explicitly included in NASA’s list of objects commonly confused with UFOs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/identifying-ufos-and-uaps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Identifying UFOs and UAPs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Identifying UFOs and UAPs</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2013</span></span></span>

Modern European rules make lighting a further identification clue rather than a complete answer. Drones intended for night operations may carry conspicuous flashing lights, including green lights on class-labelled models. Yet observers cannot safely infer that every blinking green point is a drone: conventional aircraft also carry coloured navigation lamps, and distance can merge several lights into one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/civil-drones/privacy/privacy-by-design" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: easa.europa.eu">[EASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">easa.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on europa.eu.</span></span></span>

The distinction matters most near airports and controlled airspace. Germany’s air navigation service, DFS, records drone sightings near airports and uses defined safety procedures that can include withholding take-off and landing clearances. DFS advises that suspected drones near airports or control zones should be reported promptly to the police or airport security. A report in such a setting is therefore not merely a UFO curiosity; an unidentified low-flying object may represent a real aviation hazard even when its origin is entirely human.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dfs.de/homepage/de/drohnenflug/drohnen-an-flughaefen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dfs.de">[DFS Deutsche Flugsicherung GmbH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dfs.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dfs.de.</span></span></span>

At the same time, a “drone sighting” is not automatically a confirmed drone. Night-time distance estimates are poor, small objects are difficult to track, and an observer may confuse a nearby drone with a distant aircraft or celestial light. Strong evidence would include radar or detection-system data, multiple observations from known positions, clear video containing fixed landmarks, or recovery of the device. A brief account of lights alone rarely provides that level of certainty.

## How ordinary objects become convincing UFOs

The central difficulty is not that balloons, planets or satellites resemble fictional spacecraft at close range. It is that witnesses almost never see them at close range. They see a point of light against a dark or featureless background, often for only a few seconds. With no reliable scale, the same visual angle might represent a lantern hundreds of metres away, an aircraft several kilometres away or a planet millions of kilometres away.

Several effects repeatedly strengthen a mistaken impression:

**Distance without scale.** A lone light provides almost no information about its physical size or altitude. Descriptions such as “large” or “low” may express how striking it looked rather than a measured property.

**Silence.** Lack of sound rules out less than witnesses often assume. A distant aircraft may be inaudible, a balloon or satellite produces no engine noise, and sound from a meteor may arrive well after the flash.

**Apparent hovering.** Aircraft approaching head-on, objects moving with the observer, distant planets and wind-borne lanterns can all seem stationary.

**Abrupt disappearance.** A lantern can extinguish, an aircraft can turn, a satellite can enter Earth’s shadow and a planet can pass behind cloud. The disappearance need not indicate extraordinary acceleration.

**Camera distortion.** Digital zoom, autofocus and image stabilisation can enlarge a point source into a pulsing disc or polygon. Small hand movements become dramatic when the object occupies only a few pixels, while internal lens reflections may create additional lights.

This is why the most useful first questions are practical rather than speculative: What was the precise time? Which direction was the witness facing? How long was the object visible? Did it move relative to buildings or stars? Was the light steady, flashing or streaking? Were astronomical events, satellite passes, balloon releases and known flights checked?

A report becomes more difficult to explain when those questions produce reliable, mutually consistent data and ordinary candidates fail. Most Mecklenburg-Vorpommern cases in this category do not reach that threshold. The Ludwigslust light remains plausibly attributable to a lantern-like balloon; the Neubrandenburg Halloween event closely matches a meteor; and the 2024 line of lights has a strong satellite explanation. They are valuable not as evidence of exotic visitors, but as demonstrations of how easily familiar objects can become unfamiliar in the night sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://katapult-mv.de/artikel/e-t-in-mv/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: katapult-mv.de">[katapult-mv.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">katapult-mv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span></span></span>

## What remains genuinely unresolved

Explaining common mechanisms does not justify dismissing every report in advance. “Probably a balloon” is not a satisfactory conclusion when the observation includes measured speeds, radar confirmation or movement incompatible with the wind. “Probably Venus” fails if the object crosses the sky, changes position against the stars or is observed in a direction where Venus was not present. A satellite explanation requires a path and timing that agree with an actual orbital pass.

The proper distinction is therefore between **identified**, **plausibly explained** and **unresolved** reports. The 2024 Neubrandenburg light chain is strongly identified because its arrangement and behaviour match a well-known satellite phenomenon reported across northern Germany. The 2015 fireball is strongly but not absolutely explained because trained police aviation witnesses supported the meteor interpretation, although no publicly available trajectory analysis has been located. The 2008 Ludwigslust light is weaker: a lantern fits, but the published record lacks a confirmed launch or recovered object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://katapult-mv.de/artikel/e-t-in-mv/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: katapult-mv.de">[katapult-mv.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">katapult-mv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KATAPULT MVE.T. in MV &#124; KATAPULT MV</span></span></span>

That graded approach is more useful than treating every case as either an alien craft or a complete hoax. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, ordinary aerial and astronomical objects account for much of the low-level UFO record. Learning their signatures clears away weak mysteries and leaves attention for the state’s rarer cases where multiple recordings, military activity or genuinely incomplete documentation demand closer investigation.
