## Introduction

Oskar Linke’s account entered American files through a chain of testimony, journalism, translation and bureaucratic circulation. After leaving East Germany, the former mayor of [Gleimershausen]({{ 'gleimershausen/' | relative_url }}) presented his story in West Berlin, where it was recorded as sworn testimony and publicised by the press. A Greek newspaper then printed an account on 9 July 1952. That article was translated and filed by the Central Intelligence Agency as report 00-W-23682, while versions of the same story also appeared in United States Air Force Project Blue Book material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The crucial point is that the American archival trail records the movement of Linke’s claim, not an independent American confirmation of it. The surviving CIA document identifies its source as a daily newspaper, labels the material “unevaluated information”, and contains no field investigation, technical analysis or finding that the alleged craft existed. Its historical value lies in showing why an unusual Thuringian story attracted Cold War attention and how quickly a refugee’s account could pass from local testimony into international intelligence collections.

## The West Berlin statement

Linke said that the encounter itself had occurred near Hasselbach and Gleimershausen in southern [Thuringia]({{ 'how-strong-are-thuringias-best-ufo-cases/' | relative_url }}) on 17 June 1950. Yet the story did not enter the surviving American record at that time. It became visible to Western institutions only after Linke and his family had escaped from the Soviet-controlled German Democratic Republic.

According to the later press account preserved by the CIA, Linke and his daughter made a sworn statement before a judge shortly before the article appeared. German regional broadcaster MDR similarly reports that Linke went to a Berlin notary, had his account formally certified and then made it public through the media.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That formal setting gave the story an appearance of documentary seriousness. A sworn or notarised statement establishes that a person formally made a particular claim. It does not establish that every detail in the claim is accurate. The distinction is especially important here because no surviving document shows the judge or notary independently checking the alleged landing site, interviewing unrelated witnesses in Thuringia or examining physical traces.

The wording in the American file also indicates that West Berlin intelligence officers had “begun investigating” the story. However, the preserved report does not include the results of such an investigation, identify the officers or describe any interviews they conducted beyond the reported statement. The phrase comes from the newspaper article being translated, not from a CIA investigative conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Linke’s position as an East German refugee also made the account more interesting in 1952 than an ordinary civilian sighting might have been. He said that he had originally interpreted the object as a new Soviet military machine and had been afraid to speak because of possible punishment by the authorities. The intelligence relevance, therefore, was not necessarily extraterrestrial. A report of unusual technology inside the Soviet zone could be collected because it might concern secret aircraft, guided weapons, propaganda or simply information circulating among refugees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/4 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## From a newspaper to a CIA report

The clearest surviving American document is headed **“Flying Saucers in East Germany”** and carries CIA report number **00-W-23682**. Its metadata explains precisely what kind of record it is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the country is listed as Germany;
* the subject is categorised as military and scientific;
* the material was published in a daily newspaper;
* the place of publication was Athens;
* the publication date was 9 July 1952;
* the source was the Greek newspaper *I Kathimerini*;
* the CIA distribution date was 23 August 1952;
* and, most importantly, the document is marked **“This is unevaluated information.”**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

</div>

These details show that the CIA record was part of foreign-publication monitoring. It is essentially an English-language intelligence translation or digest of a Greek newspaper article reporting a story from Berlin. It is not a CIA officer’s eyewitness report, a laboratory finding or a technical assessment of an unknown aircraft.

The chain can therefore be reconstructed cautiously:

1. Linke gave a formal account after reaching the West.
2. His story was circulated through West Berlin officials and journalists.
3. It was carried internationally and appeared in an Athens newspaper.
4. American foreign-information monitoring translated or abstracted the article.
5. The resulting document was distributed and retained in CIA records.

This route helps explain some of the documentary distance between the alleged event and the American file. The claimed encounter occurred in rural Thuringia in 1950, but the CIA document was created in 1952 from a foreign newspaper account. Every stage offered opportunities for compression, mistranslation, embellishment or the merging of details from different press reports.

The surviving text itself displays the characteristics of a news narrative rather than an investigative case file. It reproduces Linke’s detailed description of metallic-clothed figures, a large disc-like object, glowing apertures, rotation, flames and a rapid departure. It also repeats claims that local people saw something they interpreted as a low-moving comet and that Linke found a fresh circular depression at the site. Yet the document provides no names for most supposed corroborating witnesses, no photographs of the depression, no measurements taken by investigators and no contemporary report from East German authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## Intelligence and Blue Book circulation

The Linke story also became associated with Project Blue Book, the United States Air Force programme that collected and assessed reports of unidentified flying objects. MDR identifies the account as appearing both in CIA archives and in Blue Book material, while later UFO researchers have described the surviving Blue Book version as closely resembling the press account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdr.de/geschichte/ddr/alltag/erziehung-bildung/ufo-sichtung-halle-stasi-thueringen-100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdr.de">[mdr.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O-Sichtungen in der DDR &#124; mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UF O-Sichtungen in der DDR &#124; mdr.de</span></span></span>

This overlap should not be mistaken for two independent confirmations. US agencies routinely shared, copied or separately collected reports that appeared relevant to air intelligence. A single newspaper story could therefore leave records in more than one institutional collection without acquiring any additional eyewitness support.

The wider Blue Book system makes that distinction clear. The Air Force and its investigative branches received reports from military units, pilots, civilians, newspapers and foreign sources. During the early Cold War, analysts were concerned not only with unexplained phenomena but with the possibility that unusual reports might conceal Soviet aircraft, surveillance devices or weapons. The Air Force Office of Special Investigations notes that Soviet technology and espionage were central concerns during the period in which the UFO programmes operated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osi.af.mil/News/Features/Display/Article/2302429/project-blue-book-part-1-ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osi.af.mil">[OSI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osi.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

Blue Book attempted to collect technical information and assess witnesses, but the quality of individual entries depended heavily on the material supplied. A report supported by radar records, timed observations and direct interviews offered far more analytical value than a translated newspaper article describing an event said to have happened two years earlier.

The National Archives now holds the Blue Book records, including case files and administrative material preserved on 94 rolls of microfilm. Its official summary states that 12,618 sightings were reported to the programme between 1947 and 1969, with 701 remaining classified as unidentified. The same summary emphasises that “unidentified” did not mean extraterrestrial: the Air Force concluded that the programme had found no evidence of a national-security threat, unknown advanced technology or alien vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Linke’s presence in this large record system is therefore historically notable but not exceptional evidence of endorsement. Blue Book was designed to receive and sort reports. Filing was the beginning of assessment, not a declaration that a witness’s interpretation was correct.

## What the American documents do establish

The files support several limited conclusions with reasonable confidence.

**Linke’s story was circulating internationally by July 1952.** The CIA document identifies a dated Greek newspaper source and shows that the account had moved beyond West Berlin into foreign press reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

**American agencies considered it worth retaining.** Its stated military and scientific subject classifications fit the early Cold War interest in possible Soviet technology and unusual aerial reports. That explains why the story entered an intelligence collection even though it concerned a civilian claim from two years earlier.

**The story was presented as sworn testimony.** The archived article says that Linke and his daughter appeared before a judge, while MDR reports that he had the account certified through a Berlin notary before approaching the media.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

**The American version preserved a detailed narrative.** It records the alleged object’s size, shape, lights, sounds and departure, along with Linke’s belief that it might have been Soviet machinery. This makes the file useful to historians tracing how the case was framed in 1952.

**The CIA did not present the material as verified.** The explicit “unevaluated information” marking is decisive. It warns readers that the agency was circulating source material whose reliability and factual accuracy had not been established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/1 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## What the files do not establish

The archival prominence of the case can easily create a false impression of official validation. Several important forms of evidence are absent from the American documents.

There is no surviving indication that CIA personnel travelled to Gleimershausen or Hasselbach. There is no American inspection report for the alleged landing site, no soil analysis, no authenticated photograph and no technical reconstruction of the craft. The file does not contain radar observations, military tracking data or aviation records corresponding to the claimed event.

Nor does the CIA report supply a contemporary 1950 documentary trail. Its source was published in July 1952, approximately two years after the claimed sighting and after Linke had moved to the West. That delay matters because the earliest accessible account was already shaped for public circulation.

The additional witnesses mentioned in the newspaper version do not provide strong independent corroboration. They were said to have seen something resembling a comet at a distance, not the landed craft and occupants described by Linke. The CIA text gives little information with which to identify, interview or compare their accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlying_Saucers_in_East_Germany%2C_CIA_Report.pdf/4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:Flying Saucers in East Germany, CIA Report.pdf/4 - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Even the basic narrative developed over time. Later UFO literature supplied the exact 17 June 1950 date and added or elaborated details that were not always presented consistently in the first international reports. A sceptical review preserved by the National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena archive notes that later researchers contacted Linke and incorporated information from additional clippings and interviews. That expanding record may show that Linke maintained his story, but it also makes it difficult to separate his earliest recollection from details added during years of retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/500617hasselbach_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

MDR has also raised the possibility that the timing of Linke’s public statement was influenced by the growing flying-saucer culture of 1952, including the West German release of the science-fiction film *The Day the Earth Stood Still*. This does not demonstrate invention. It does, however, identify a plausible cultural setting in which an old and initially military-sounding memory could be reframed as a flying-saucer encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdr.de/geschichte/ddr/alltag/erziehung-bildung/ufo-sichtung-halle-stasi-thueringen-100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdr.de">[mdr.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O-Sichtungen in der DDR &#124; mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UF O-Sichtungen in der DDR &#124; mdr.de</span></span></span>

## Filing a claim versus confirming it

The Linke case illustrates a recurring problem in the interpretation of intelligence archives. Official documents can authenticate the history of a story without authenticating the event described by that story.

The CIA file is genuine evidence that American intelligence collected an article about Linke. It is also genuine evidence of the article’s date, source, bureaucratic classification and wording. It is not equivalent to evidence that intelligence officers confirmed a 15-metre craft, two occupants or physical traces in a Thuringian field.

The distinction becomes clearer when the stages are separated:

**The underlying claim:** Linke and his daughter allegedly saw an extraordinary object in 1950.

**The formal statement:** Linke later placed the account before a judge or notary in West Berlin.

**The media report:** Newspapers publicised the statement as a potentially important flying-saucer or Soviet-secret-weapon story.

**The intelligence record:** The CIA translated and distributed one foreign newspaper version as unevaluated information.

**The later archive:** Declassification made the intelligence copy available to historians, researchers and UFO writers.

Only the last four stages are firmly documented by the surviving archival trail. The first—the physical encounter itself—remains dependent mainly on Linke’s testimony.

For Thuringia’s UFO history, this is precisely why the case matters. It demonstrates how the political geography of divided Germany could transform a delayed rural sighting claim into material of international interest. A former East German mayor, a story about possible Soviet machinery, West Berlin intelligence attention and the global flying-saucer wave of 1952 together made the account collectable.

The American files therefore strengthen the case’s historical significance but not its physical evidence. They show that Linke’s story travelled remarkably far. They do not show that the alleged craft was ever independently verified.
