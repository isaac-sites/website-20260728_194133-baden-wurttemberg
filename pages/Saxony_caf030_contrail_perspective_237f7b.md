## Introduction

A level-flying aircraft can look as though it is climbing almost vertically when it travels broadly towards or away from the observer. The aircraft is moving across three-dimensional space, but the eye sees only its changing position on a two-dimensional sky. When most of the movement is along the observer’s line of sight, the sideways component becomes compressed, making a long, nearly horizontal contrail appear steep, curved or even upright.

This perspective effect offers the most plausible explanation for the widely publicised photographs from Aue in [Saxony]({{ 'why-saxonys-ufo-history-remains-so/' | relative_url }})’s Erzgebirge region. ## How viewing angle changes apparent direction

The simplest comparison is a straight railway line. The rails remain parallel, yet they appear to meet in the distance because their separation is compressed into an increasingly small part of the observer’s field of view. A high-altitude aircraft flying roughly towards a person produces a related effect: much of its actual forward movement is directed towards the observer rather than visibly across the sky.

The contrail marks where the aircraft has already been. From the ground, the distant end may appear close to the horizon while the aircraft at the nearer end appears higher in the sky. Joining those points visually produces an apparent upward slope. If the route is aligned especially closely with the observer, the trail can look almost vertical even though the aircraft is maintaining an ordinary cruising altitude.

The illusion becomes stronger because observers usually lack reliable depth cues in an open sky. There are no nearby buildings, trees or mountains at the aircraft’s altitude against which to judge distance. A tiny bright point may therefore be interpreted as a nearby object climbing rapidly rather than a large aircraft approaching from many kilometres away.

Several features can reinforce the wrong interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Foreshortening:** A trail extending mainly towards the observer appears much shorter than its true length.
* **Apparent acceleration:** As an approaching aircraft covers the final part of its visible route, its angular movement across the sky can change noticeably even if its actual speed remains steady.
* **Reversed direction:** The broad, older end of a trail may look like the near end, encouraging the observer to imagine that the object is travelling away and upwards when it is actually approaching.
* **Loss of the horizon:** Photographs framed tightly around the trail remove landscape references that might reveal its true orientation.
* **Camera tilt:** A slightly rotated phone or camera can turn a steep-looking diagonal into an apparently vertical plume.

</div>

These effects do not require poor eyesight or an unreliable witness. They arise because the scene itself contains too little depth information for the brain to reconstruct the geometry confidently. A similar head-on contrail illusion caused a highly publicised “mystery missile” report off California in 2010. Subsequent analysis found no corresponding fast, unidentified radar target, and US authorities concluded that the feature was most probably an aircraft condensation trail seen from a deceptive angle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

## Why a straight trail can look curved

A contrail is not a rigid structure attached to an aircraft. It is a cloud of ice crystals left behind in moving air. The newest section remains close to the aircraft’s recent path, while older sections have had more time to spread, drift and deform.

Upper-level winds can differ in speed and direction across relatively small changes in altitude. This wind shear can bend or displace older portions of a trail. Wake vortices behind the wings may also twist the newly formed trail before it begins to diffuse. From an oblique viewpoint, relatively modest distortions can appear to form a hook, corkscrew or sudden turn.

The Federal Aviation Administration notes that persistent contrails may remain for hours, spread with upper-atmospheric winds and drift far from the aircraft that formed them. It also explains that trails may appear to begin or end abruptly when an aircraft enters or leaves a sufficiently cold, humid pocket of air. Such discontinuities can make an ordinary flight look as though an object has switched its exhaust on, changed power or performed a sudden manoeuvre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/contrails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span></span></span>

This matters when interpreting still photographs. A single image freezes together parts of the trail of different ages. The broad, wind-disturbed section may have been formed many minutes before the narrow segment beside the aircraft. Treating the entire shape as a simultaneous record of the aircraft’s attitude can therefore create a false impression of a looping or sharply climbing path.

## The role of low sunlight and distance

Contrail reports become especially dramatic near sunrise or sunset. An observer may already be in shadow while an aircraft several kilometres above the ground remains illuminated by direct sunlight. Its trail can therefore shine white, yellow, orange or red against a darker sky.

Contrails consist mainly of ice crystals produced when moist aircraft exhaust mixes with sufficiently cold air. The German Air Force states that they commonly form at roughly 8,000 to 12,000 metres, where temperatures may be between about −40°C and −50°C. Humid air allows them to persist and spread, while dry air causes them to disappear more quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundeswehr.de/de/organisation/luftwaffe/aktuelles/mythos-kondensstreifen-die-luftwaffe-klaert-auf-5474662" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundeswehr.de">[Bundeswehr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundeswehr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mythos Kondensstreifen: die Luftwaffe klärt auf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mythos Kondensstreifen: die Luftwaffe klärt auf...</span></span></span>

Low-angle light changes more than colour. It can:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* make the aircraft itself appear as a bright point resembling a flame;
* illuminate one side of the trail while leaving the other side grey or dark;
* exaggerate ridges and twists within a spreading trail;
* make a distant trail visible over a far greater length than it would be at midday;
* produce the impression that smoke or fire is pouring from the object.

</div>

The FAA records that contrails may appear white, grey, red, orange or yellow as sunlight reflects from their ice crystals. The Met Office likewise notes that persistent trails can spread for hours, show strong optical colours and cast shadows on thinner cloud layers below.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/contrails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span></span></span>

A recent Saxon example shows how persuasive this combination can be. In November 2025, reports of an apparently falling or burning object near Schöneck in the Vogtland prompted a large search. Photographs were later assessed as showing the disturbed contrails of two aircraft, lit from below by the Sun so that they looked fiery. Police described the event as an optical illusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdr.de/nachrichten/sachsen/chemnitz/vogtland/flugobjekt-unbekannt-absturz-suche-polizei-102.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdr.de">[mdr.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ufo-Meldestelle liefert Lösung für Himmels-Rätsel im Vogtland &#124; mdr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ufo-Meldestelle liefert Lösung für Himmels-Rätsel im Vogtland &#124; mdr.de</span></span></span>

That incident was not identical to the Aue sighting, but it demonstrates the same broader lesson: unusual geometry and low sunlight can transform ordinary aircraft trails into scenes suggesting fire, descent or impossible movement.

A military-aircraft suggestion may have sounded persuasive because combat aircraft can climb more steeply than airliners. Yet invoking a steep climb may not be necessary at all. The more cautious assessment is therefore that the Aue images are consistent with an aircraft contrail seen under deceptive viewing and lighting conditions, while the surviving public evidence is insufficient to identify the precise flight with certainty.

## Practical checks for identifying aircraft trails

A convincing assessment requires more than asking whether the feature “looks like” a contrail. The most useful checks reconstruct the observation rather than relying on visual resemblance alone.

**Establish the exact time and position.** Even an uncertainty of several minutes can produce the wrong aircraft match. Investigators should preserve the original photograph or video because its metadata may contain a timestamp and sometimes location information.

**Recover the viewing direction.** Buildings, hills, street alignments and distinctive horizon features can show where the camera was pointing. In the Erzgebirge, uneven terrain makes this particularly important: a ridge may conceal the true horizon and exaggerate the apparent angle of a trail.

**Check the Sun’s position.** A trail glowing orange or apparently burning is far less mysterious when the Sun was low or already below the observer’s local horizon. High-altitude objects can remain sunlit after the ground has entered dusk.

**Watch the aircraft’s motion.** A rocket-like object generally changes position and apparent size differently from a distant airliner. Slow angular movement, followed by a gradual change in direction as the aircraft approaches or passes, supports an aircraft interpretation.

**Observe how the trail evolves.** Fresh contrail should form behind the moving point. Older parts may widen, drift or twist. A plume that spreads slowly while the bright point continues steadily is more consistent with ice cloud than with a solid object or sustained flame.

**Compare several frames, not one.** A single image cannot establish whether the trail is vertical in space. A longer video may show that the apparent “climb” is mainly an aircraft advancing towards the observer.

**Use flight-tracking records carefully.** Modern tracking services can be helpful, but absence from a public display is not proof that no aircraft was present. Coverage can be incomplete, some military flights may be filtered, timestamps may be wrong, and persistent trails may drift far from the aircraft that created them. The FAA specifically cautions that old contrails can become difficult or impossible to match to a particular flight using public tracking sites alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/contrails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Contrails &#124; Federal Aviation Administration</span></span></span>

**Look for corroboration.** A genuinely exceptional climb over a populated area would be stronger if recorded independently from separated locations. Two viewing positions allow investigators to compare angles and estimate the object’s three-dimensional path. One distant photograph cannot do that reliably.

## What the illusion changes about Saxony’s UFO record

Contrail perspective does not explain every unusual light or object reported in Saxony. It does, however, weaken claims based mainly on the apparent steepness, verticality or curvature of an aircraft-like trail. Those qualities cannot be read directly as measurements of flight performance.

The Aue sighting matters because it shows the gap between a witness’s understandable visual impression and what a photograph can establish. The image may genuinely look like an object making an impossible climb. Yet without distance information, that “climb” may be a horizontal route aimed towards the observer; without continuous footage, the “curve” may be wind distortion; and without lighting analysis, the apparent flame may be reflected low sunlight.

The most defensible classification is therefore neither a dismissive claim that nothing unusual was seen nor an assertion that extraordinary manoeuvring occurred. An unusual appearance was recorded, but ordinary aviation geometry offers a strong mechanism for producing it. In evidence-led terms, the Aue photographs support a plausible contrail explanation more strongly than they support an aircraft performing an impossible ascent.
