## Introduction

[Schleswig-Holstein]({{ 'what-is-really-seen-over-schleswig/' | relative_url }})’s UFO record is not held in one official file series. It is divided among civilian investigation groups, online databases, local newspaper archives, witness correspondence and the private working files of individual researchers. Germany has never maintained a prominent public, state-by-state UFO investigation service comparable with France’s official system, and a Bundestag research paper found no legal obligation for the federal government to collect or investigate UFO reports. This fragmented arrangement explains both the strengths and weaknesses of the surviving evidence. Civilian investigators have preserved thousands of reports that might otherwise have disappeared, sometimes using questionnaires, image analysis and astronomical checks. Yet no organisation receives every sighting, applies exactly the same classifications or makes every case file public. Schleswig-Holstein therefore has collections of records, not a complete UFO chronology.

## Civilian groups do most of the investigative work

The principal custodians of German UFO reports are private organisations rather than government departments. The Society for the Investigation of the UFO Phenomenon, generally known by its German initials GEP, has investigated reports since 1972. Its work includes structured witness interviews, photographic examination, comparison with astronomical and aviation information, and the recording of conclusions in a case database. Its published dataset contains fields such as the date, time and location of an observation, the route by which it was reported, a free-text description, classification and investigation result. Personal details are removed from the public version for privacy reasons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–202324 Jan 2024 — Die Falldaten der Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens (GEP) e.V. enthalten It...</span></span></span>

That is valuable for Schleswig-Holstein because it creates a searchable national framework into which reports from the state can be placed. A sighting from Rendsburg need not survive solely as a clipped newspaper story: it may also acquire a case number, an investigator’s assessment and a classification that can be compared with reports elsewhere. The GEP has periodically released updated datasets through the Zenodo research repository, improving preservation and allowing independent users to examine the non-personal case information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/13923653" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–2024.0912 Oct 2024 — Die Falldaten der Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens (GEP) e.V. enthalten...</span></span></span>

CENAP, the Central Research Network for Extraordinary Celestial Phenomena, performs a similar public-facing role with a more explicitly sceptical reputation. Founded in 1976, it receives reports, compares them with common sources of misidentification and maintains an extensive online archive of case discussions, reference material and historic publications. Its investigators frequently attribute reports to bright planets, meteors, aircraft, satellites, balloons or other conventional causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deutschland.de/en/topic/knowledge/ufos-over-germany-hansjuergen-koehler-and-cenap" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deutschland.de">[Deutschland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deutschland.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos over germany hansjuergen koehler and cenap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs over Germany: An expert provides clarity29 Nov 2024 — UFOs over Germany: Read on to learn how Hansjürgen Köhler and the C...</span></span></span>

Other civilian bodies, including the German-speaking Society for UFO Research and MUFON-CES, have also accepted witness reports. The availability of several reporting channels is useful for witnesses who might distrust one organisation or prefer a different investigative approach. It also creates duplication and inconsistency. One event may be reported to several groups under different dates or descriptions, while another may remain in the private inbox of a single investigator. MUFON-CES, for example, has offered a questionnaire and telephone reporting line, illustrating how much the national record depends on voluntary submission routes rather than automatic official collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/kurz-notiert/mufon-ces-mit-neuer-webseite-und-thema-volksaufklaerung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

The groups do not always agree about methods or interpretation. Some lean strongly towards conventional explanations; others are more willing to treat a small residue of cases as evidence of a genuinely anomalous phenomenon. Their categories may also differ. “Unexplained” can mean that a thorough investigation found no satisfactory answer, but it can also mean that the witness supplied too little information for a confident identification. Those two outcomes have very different evidential weight and should not be merged when assessing Schleswig-Holstein’s history.

## A Rendsburg photograph shows the system in practice

A reported photograph from Rendsburg offers a useful example of how the civilian system works. A woman circulated an image showing a blurred object that she had apparently not noticed while taking the photograph. The regional newspaper *Kieler Nachrichten* consulted the GEP, which could not identify the object confidently from the material available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kn-online.de/lokales/rendsburg-eckernfoerde/das-steckt-hinter-einer-ufo-sichtung-ueber-rendsburg-275TAQRGARCXXPPUDOIRQC33VY.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kn-online.de">[KN – Kieler Nachrichten]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kn-online.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kn-online.de.</span></span></span>

The result was not a confirmation of an extraordinary craft. It was a recognition that a single blurred photograph may not contain enough information to distinguish between a distant aircraft, a bird, an insect close to the lens, airborne debris or a motion artefact. A rigorous inquiry would ideally need the original image file, camera settings, exact time, viewing direction, weather conditions, neighbouring frames and independent witnesses. Without these, even a competent private investigator may have to leave the case open.

This distinction is easily lost when a local article or social-media post is separated from the investigator’s full reasoning. “Not identified” may be repeated years later as “unexplainable”, and “unexplainable” may then be presented as “evidence of a UFO craft”. The Rendsburg case matters less for what the object was than for what it reveals about record survival: the public may see a photograph and a brief newspaper quotation, while the underlying exchange, metadata and analytical notes may remain incomplete or unavailable.

Local journalism can nevertheless strengthen an investigation. A newspaper can establish when an image first appeared, contact the photographer, find additional witnesses and ask specialists to comment. It can also preserve a public date-stamped account after a social-media post has been deleted. The weakness is that articles are written for news value, not as permanent scientific case reports. Details may be shortened, images compressed and unresolved findings turned into striking headlines.

## Local press is both archive and filter

Much of Schleswig-Holstein’s older UFO history is likely to survive in regional newspapers rather than specialist databases. Papers serving Kiel, Lübeck, Flensburg, Rendsburg, Neumünster and the coastal districts routinely recorded unusual lights, alleged falling objects, meteor displays and public speculation. These reports can reveal the original language used by witnesses, whether police or airports received calls, and whether several communities described the same event.

They can also reveal how an apparent local mystery was resolved. A widely seen glowing object reported across northern Germany in October 2009 generated calls from Schleswig-Holstein and neighbouring regions. Police found no evidence of an aircraft crash, while the available information supported a meteor explanation. A researcher studying only a clipping headed “UFO seen over northern Germany” could miss the later clarification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thelocal.de/20091014/22559" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelocal.de">[The Local Germany]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelocal.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Local Germany Multiple witnesses spot UFO in northern Germany</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Local Germany Multiple witnesses spot UFO in northern Germany</span></span></span>

Newspaper evidence has several recurring limitations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Uneven preservation:** some titles and years are digitised, while others remain on microfilm, in bound volumes or behind commercial search systems.
* **Changing vocabulary:** older reports may use terms such as “flying saucer”, “mysterious light”, “fireball” or “celestial phenomenon” rather than “UFO”, making simple keyword searches incomplete.
* **Repetition:** several newspapers may reproduce the same agency report, creating the illusion of numerous independent accounts.
* **Missing follow-ups:** the dramatic initial report is more likely to be indexed or remembered than a short correction printed days later.
* **Loss of source material:** photographs printed in newspapers may be cropped, screened or heavily compressed, preventing meaningful modern analysis.

</div>

Historical newspaper portals can improve access, but digitisation is not the same as completeness. Searchable newspaper systems typically depend on optical character recognition, which can misread old typefaces, damaged pages, place names and unusual vocabulary. Research on digital newspaper interfaces warns that search tools and collection design shape what users are able to find. A failed keyword search therefore does not prove that no report was published.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zefys.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zefys.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de">[zefys.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zefys.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on staatsbibliothek-berlin.de.</span></span></span>

For Schleswig-Holstein, a serious chronology would require more than searching current newspaper websites. It would involve regional editions, library holdings, municipal archives and variant terminology, followed by checks for subsequent explanations. That work has not been completed in a single public resource.

## Private archives preserve evidence but restrict access

The most detailed records are often not the most accessible. A civilian case file may contain the witness’s original letter, questionnaire answers, hand-drawn sketches, correspondence with airports, photographic negatives and an investigator’s working notes. Public databases usually expose only selected fields or a shortened case summary.

The GEP maintains both working records and archival material, including paper case files, books, journals and newspaper cuttings. Its move towards digitised datasets is an important step, but privacy rules properly limit the release of witness identities and potentially identifying details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO / UAP Falldaten 1972–202324 Jan 2024 — Die Falldaten der Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens (GEP) e.V. enthalten It...</span></span></span>

CENAP’s archive illustrates another form of preservation. Its historic reports and case commentary have been progressively placed online, and reporting in 2019 noted that more than four decades of material were being made publicly available. The online collection includes case discussions and long-running serial publications, allowing readers to see how explanations and investigative habits changed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.welt.de/wissenschaft/article188160697/Mysterioese-Flugobjekte-Ufo-Meldestelle-stellt-ihr-Archiv-online.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: welt.de">[DIE WELT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">welt.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DIE WELTMysteriöse Flugobjekte: „Ufo-Meldestelle“ stellt ihr Archiv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DIE WELTMysteriöse Flugobjekte: „Ufo-Meldestelle“ stellt ihr Archiv</span></span></span>

These efforts reduce the risk that entire collections will vanish when a researcher retires or dies. They do not eliminate it. Private archives depend on volunteer labour, website maintenance, storage space, copyright permissions and decisions about what deserves digitisation. A broken website, obsolete file format or unlabelled box of correspondence can remove material from practical use even when it technically still exists.

There is also an unavoidable selection effect. Civilian archives contain reports that reached the organisation. They do not record every unusual observation made in Schleswig-Holstein. Witnesses may tell the police, a newspaper, friends or nobody at all. Others may submit the same report decades later, after memory has changed. Reporting totals therefore measure contact with a particular organisation as well as the underlying frequency of unusual observations.

This is especially important when annual figures are used to claim a “wave”. CENAP recorded 19 reports from Schleswig-Holstein in 2023, according to regional reporting, but that number cannot be treated as a complete census of everything seen in the state. It reflects the reports received and retained by that network during that year. Public awareness, media attention, satellite launches and the ease of online submission can all alter the count.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtl.de/rtl-nord/deutschland-karte-zeigt-ufo-sichtungen-bekommen-wir-besuch-aus-dem-all-id1899914.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtl.de">[rtl.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtl.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">deutschland karte zeigt ufo sichtungen bekommen wir besuch aus dem all id1899914</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">deutschland karte zeigt ufo sichtungen bekommen wir besuch aus dem all id1899914</span></span></span>

## Official archives hold fragments, not a dedicated UFO collection

The absence of a specialist state investigation programme does not mean that public bodies hold no relevant records. Police call logs, aviation correspondence, military documents, parliamentary papers, civil-defence files and municipal records may contain references to unexplained objects. The problem is that these records were created for other administrative purposes and are filed according to the responsible authority, date and function rather than under a unified UFO heading.

The Federal Archives preserves records produced by federal institutions, while the Schleswig-Holstein State Archives preserves material relating to the state’s administration and history. Neither is presented as a comprehensive Schleswig-Holstein UFO repository. A researcher would need to know which authority might have received a report and then identify the relevant file series.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundesarchiv.de/en/federal-archives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundesarchiv.de">[Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundesarchiv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bundesarchiv.de.</span></span></span>

This creates several practical obstacles. A sighting near a naval facility might be recorded in military security correspondence, a police incident file or not at all. A reported object near an airport might be absorbed into ordinary aviation-safety paperwork without the term “UFO”. Records containing personal information may remain restricted. Routine call logs may be destroyed after retention periods expire, while significant incidents may be retained under a broader subject such as air safety, public order or defence.

The Bundestag papers sometimes described online as “German UFO files” illustrate how easily the nature of a document can be overstated. The papers were parliamentary research studies concerning extraterrestrial life, international policy and unidentified objects; they were not the product of a national case-investigation programme. The contrast with countries that created designated UFO file series is instructive. Britain’s National Archives can direct researchers to specific defence, air force, foreign-office and parliamentary collections, while the United States National Archives now provides a dedicated UAP records portal and bulk downloads. Germany’s relevant material is generally less centralised and less consistently labelled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why no complete state chronology exists

A definitive year-by-year list would require several record systems to be combined, de-duplicated and reassessed. No existing archive fully performs that task for Schleswig-Holstein.

The central problems are structural:

**No universal reporting route.** A resident can contact the GEP, CENAP, another private group, a newspaper, the police or an airport. There is no rule requiring those bodies to forward every report to one central repository.

**Different case standards.** One organisation may open a file from a brief online message, while another may require a completed questionnaire. Some databases include dreams, alleged encounters or photographs discovered later; others concentrate on observed aerial objects. Totals may therefore measure different things.

**Incomplete geographical tagging.** A light seen over the Baltic may be reported from a Schleswig-Holstein beach but located over Danish or international waters. Conversely, an event physically over the state may be logged under the observer’s location in Hamburg or Lower Saxony.

**Duplicate reports.** A meteor, rocket re-entry or satellite train can generate calls from many towns. Those accounts may represent one physical event, dozens of witness reports or several database entries.

**Delayed identification.** An investigator may initially classify a case as unexplained and revise it after receiving flight data, astronomical calculations or another photograph. Copies of the earlier classification may continue circulating elsewhere.

**Privacy and access restrictions.** Public datasets exclude personal details, while full files may be closed or available only by request. This protects witnesses but limits independent checking of credibility, chronology and possible collaboration between observers.

**Archive loss.** Private correspondence, local editions, photographic negatives and early digital files are vulnerable to disposal, decay and technological obsolescence.

These issues do not make historical research impossible. They mean that any count should be described as a minimum, a sample or a record of reports received by named organisations—not as the total number of UFO events in Schleswig-Holstein.

## How to judge records from fragmented collections

Readers can still distinguish stronger entries from weak ones by asking how much of the original evidence survives and whether different archives support each other.

A comparatively strong historical file would contain a contemporaneous witness statement, an exact location and time, original photographs or negatives, weather and astronomical checks, information from aviation or police sources, and a documented conclusion. Independent descriptions made before witnesses influenced one another would add value. A clear explanation reached through these records strengthens the historical account just as much as an unresolved conclusion.

A weaker entry may consist only of a later recollection, an undated clipping or a copied database summary. Such material can show that a story circulated, but not necessarily that the reported event occurred exactly as described. A case becomes particularly unreliable when all online versions can be traced back to the same short newspaper paragraph or anonymous submission.

The word “unresolved” should therefore be reserved for cases with enough information to test several ordinary explanations but no satisfactory result. Where crucial information is absent, “insufficient data” is more accurate. Contemporary research on anomalous aerial reports increasingly stresses the need for curated data, standardised metadata and multiple forms of observation because isolated witness narratives and images are difficult to verify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

For Schleswig-Holstein, the best interpretations often emerge by linking different record types. A private investigator’s case summary may be compared with local weather data, press coverage, astronomical records and official confirmation that no aviation incident occurred. No single archive is automatically authoritative; the value lies in agreement between records created independently for different purposes.

## The archive gap is part of the state’s UFO history

Private investigators have prevented Schleswig-Holstein’s UFO record from disappearing. Their questionnaires, databases, journals and correspondence preserve ordinary misidentifications alongside a smaller number of cases that remain unresolved or poorly documented. Local newspapers add dates, public reaction and occasional official comment, while public archives may retain fragments generated through policing, aviation or administration.

The same system also limits what can responsibly be claimed. There is no complete state register, no stable denominator for calculating sighting rates and no guarantee that two organisations use “unexplained” in the same way. Apparent clusters may reflect publicity or reporting access as much as unusual activity in the sky.

The most defensible account of Schleswig-Holstein’s UFO history is therefore archival rather than sensational. It is a history assembled from overlapping collections, each preserving part of the picture and each introducing its own gaps. Civilian investigation has created a substantial body of evidence, but the surviving record supports careful case-by-case assessment—not claims that the state possesses a hidden, continuous or uniquely extraordinary UFO chronology.
