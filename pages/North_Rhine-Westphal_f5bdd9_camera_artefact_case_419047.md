## Introduction

A photograph can make a weak sighting look stronger than it was. A witness in North Rhine-Westphalia may notice nothing unusual while taking a landscape picture, then later discover a dark disc, bright orb or elongated “craft” in one frame. The object may appear sharply bounded when enlarged, even though it was probably a nearby insect, an out-of-focus light, a reflection inside the lens or a pattern created by digital compression.

These cases matter because photographs are often treated as independent confirmation of a UFO report. In reality, the camera may have produced the mystery rather than recorded what the witness saw. This is a recurring issue for the Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens (GEP), the civilian investigation organisation based in Lüdenscheid. ## How nearby insects become disc-shaped blurs

The most misleading “UFO” in a daylight photograph may be only a few centimetres from the camera. A fly, bee or other small insect passing close to the lens can occupy a large part of the frame while remaining far outside the camera’s focus range. Its wings and body merge into a soft oval, spindle or disc, sometimes with a darker centre and lighter edges.

Motion adds another distortion. During the fraction of a second in which the photograph is exposed, the insect continues moving and beating its wings. Instead of recording a recognisable animal, the camera combines its changing positions into one blurred form. In video, the exposure time of each frame can produce an apparently long body with repeated side projections—the effect popularly described as a “rod” or “skyfish”. Such forms are widely understood as insects recorded with motion blur rather than a previously unknown class of flying object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_%28optical_phenomenon%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rod (optical phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rod (optical phenomenon</span></span></span>

Distance is especially easy to misjudge because an isolated object against a blue or cloudy sky offers few scale clues. A two-centimetre insect very close to the lens can cover the same number of pixels as a large object much farther away. Once the insect has been defocused, its familiar anatomical details disappear, encouraging the viewer to interpret the remaining silhouette as a solid craft.

A useful example comes directly from the GEP’s investigative work. Its 5,000th registered report, received in June 2022, concerned an object found only after a landscape photograph and short iPhone Live Photo were reviewed. The witness thought the object seemed to cross the scene at extreme speed. GEP investigators identified it as an insect. The incident occurred outside North Rhine-Westphalia, but it is relevant to the state’s UFO history because the analysis was conducted by the Lüdenscheid-based organisation that receives and assesses many reports from the state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/verein/gep-untersuchte-5-000-ufo-fall" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gep untersuchte 5 000 ufo fall</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ForschungGEP untersuchte 5.000. The case illustrates a common warning sign: the photographer did not see the object at the time. That does not automatically prove an artefact, but it weakens claims about its apparent size, distance, sound or behaviour. A person who notices an object only during later enlargement cannot provide an independent visual estimate of where it was in three-dimensional space.

## Reflections, focus errors and digital compression

Not every misleading form is a physical object in front of the camera. Bright lights can reflect between the optical elements inside a lens, producing spots, discs, arcs or coloured patches that were never present in the scene. These “lens flare” artefacts are particularly likely when the Sun, a streetlight or another intense source is inside the frame or just beyond its edge. Optical research describes flare spots as non-image information formed by internal reflections within the camera system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04384" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Automatic Flare Spot Artifact Detection and Removal in Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Automatic Flare Spot Artifact Detection and Removal in PhotographsMarch 7, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 7, 2021</span></span></span>

A reflection may appear on the opposite side of the image from the bright source or move when the camera moves. In night photographs, a lamp can generate a pale green or purple “orb” against an otherwise dark sky. Cropping the photograph may conceal the light responsible, leaving the reflection to look like an isolated luminous object. Investigators therefore need the entire frame, not just an enlargement of the supposed UFO.

Focus errors can create an even more structured appearance. An out-of-focus point of light is rendered as a blur whose shape is influenced by the lens aperture. Depending on the optical design, it may appear circular, polygonal or irregular rather than star-like. Photographers know this effect as bokeh. The shape is a property of the imaging system, not evidence that the original light source had the same outline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nikon.co.uk/en_GB/learn-and-explore/magazine/tips-and-tricks/what-is-bokeh-and-how-should-i-use-it?srsltid=AfmBOoohnjU_MaUMY5UzF98VR9hKuXs4Kgvortj56jVopUduxIIO7NPv" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nikon.co.uk">[Nikon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nikon.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nikon Bokeh for beginners</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bokeh for beginnersMay 15, 2026 — 15 May 2026 — Bokeh refers to the aesthetic quality of the blur in the out-of-focus areas of a pho...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2026</span></span></span>

This becomes important when witnesses photograph Venus, an aircraft light or another bright point using digital zoom. The enlarged image may show a disc with rings, flat sides or apparent internal divisions. Autofocus can “hunt” against a featureless sky, while atmospheric movement and camera shake alter the shape from frame to frame. The resulting object looks more detailed than it truly is because the detail comes from diffraction, defocus, sharpening and image processing.

Modern phones add further layers of computation. They may combine several exposures, reduce noise, sharpen edges, brighten shadows or simulate shallow depth of field. These operations usually improve ordinary photographs, but they can turn a tiny ambiguous patch into something that appears bounded and mechanical. Apple, for example, notes that camera settings and other capture information are stored with photographs, while some modes preserve additional focus or depth data that affect how an image is rendered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/see-photo-and-video-information-iph0edb9c18f/ios" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: support.apple.com">[Apple Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">support.apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apple.com.</span></span></span>

Compression then removes information. JPEG encoding simplifies fine detail so that files occupy less storage space. Repeated saving, messaging or social-media uploading can introduce block patterns, ringing around sharp boundaries and false edges. A few uncertain pixels may become a dark central “dome”, a light rim or a row of apparently regular openings once the image is enlarged far beyond its original resolution.

This does not mean that every strange-looking photograph is worthless. It means that visual complexity is not the same as physical detail. NASA’s independent UAP study noted that several apparent anomalies have proved to be sensor artefacts once calibration and metadata were examined. ## Why screenshots and enlargements mislead

Online UFO discussions often begin with the weakest available version of an image: a screenshot of a social-media post, a cropped enlargement or a frame extracted from a compressed video. Each step separates the picture from information needed to interpret it.

Cropping removes environmental clues. Investigators may lose the position of the Sun, nearby windows, overhead wires, trees, birds or aircraft navigation lights. Enlargement does not restore missing detail; it simply makes the existing pixels larger. Sharpening can then emphasise compression boundaries until they resemble panels, lights or a metallic outline.

A single frame can also exaggerate movement. An insect that appears in only one image may be described as a craft travelling too rapidly to see, when it may simply have crossed the small volume of air close to the lens. Conversely, an object that shifts position between frames may appear to make a dramatic manoeuvre because the camera itself moved. Without stable landmarks or recorded orientation, separating object movement from camera movement is difficult.

The same caution applies to video. The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office has noted that publicly circulated or operational video may be compressed and may lack the metadata needed for exhaustive analysis. In its examination of the “GoFast” footage, missing georeferenced sensor information limited what could initially be calculated; reconstructing the geometry substantially reduced the impression of extraordinary speed. Although that military case is not from North Rhine-Westphalia, the analytical lesson applies directly to local phone footage: apparent velocity cannot be calculated reliably without distance, viewing angle, camera movement and accurate timing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Go Fast Case Resolution</span></span></span>

## Why original files matter to investigators

The original file is not magically truthful, but it contains more evidence than a screenshot or repost. It may preserve the full image dimensions, exact capture time, device model, lens choice, exposure settings and other Exchangeable Image File Format information, usually called EXIF metadata. These details can help determine whether a small object was blurred by a slow shutter speed, whether digital zoom was used, or whether the photograph was edited and exported before submission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/see-photo-and-video-information-iph0edb9c18f/ios" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: support.apple.com">[Apple Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">support.apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apple.com.</span></span></span>

Investigators usually need more than one file. The strongest submission includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the untouched photograph or video directly from the device;
* the frames immediately before and after the anomaly;
* the uncropped scene and any wider photographs taken from the same position;
* the exact time and location;
* the direction in which the camera was pointing;
* the phone or camera model and selected mode;
* an account of what was seen with the naked eye, separately from what appeared later in the image.

</div>

Sequence information is often decisive. A blurred oval that changes shape greatly between adjacent frames is compatible with an insect or focus artefact. A reflection may shift in relation to a bright light as the camera angle changes. A distant physical object should behave consistently with the scene’s geometry, even if its identity remains unknown.

Preserving the original also helps distinguish ordinary editing from possible manipulation. Professional forensic guidance treats the source file, associated metadata and documented handling history as central to image authentication. Good practice is to retain an untouched copy and conduct analysis on duplicates rather than repeatedly resaving the only available version.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-investigations-digital-imaging-and-multimedia-procedure/digital-imaging-and-multimedia-procedure-v30" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">digital imaging and multimedia procedure v30</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">digital imaging and multimedia procedure v30</span></span></span>

Sharing methods matter as well. Some platforms resize photographs, recompress them or remove metadata. Apple’s own guidance distinguishes between ordinary sharing and transferring an item in its original format with associated data. Once a picture has passed through several messaging applications, investigators may be unable to recover settings that would have explained the anomaly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://support.apple.com/en-ge/guide/iphone/iph1c81f302d/ios" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: support.apple.com">[Apple Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">support.apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apple.com.</span></span></span>

Metadata must nevertheless be treated as supporting evidence rather than proof. Dates and locations can sometimes be altered, and the absence of metadata may result from routine sharing rather than deception. Image analysis is strongest when technical information agrees with the witness’s account, the surrounding frames and independent observations.

## What makes a photograph genuinely useful

A compelling-looking enlargement is not necessarily a strong UFO case. The evidential value of an image depends less on how dramatic the object appears than on whether the circumstances allow alternative explanations to be tested.

A photograph becomes more useful when the object was seen before the camera was raised; several witnesses independently described it; multiple uncropped frames show consistent motion; landmarks permit angular measurements; and the original files retain reliable timestamps and camera data. Separate recordings from different locations are especially valuable because they can establish distance through triangulation and rule out insects or reflections confined to one camera.

Purpose-built research systems therefore use several calibrated sensors rather than relying on one attractive image. Proposed scientific UAP observatories combine wide- and narrow-field cameras with radar, radio, acoustic and environmental instruments. The aim is not merely to obtain a clearer photograph, but to establish range, trajectory and physical characteristics while recognising artefacts through independent measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

For North Rhine-Westphalia, this helps explain why many photographic reports do not become landmark incidents. The state has dense urban populations, heavy [air traffic]({{ 'air-traffic/' | relative_url }}) and an experienced civilian investigation centre, but a surprising shape in one compressed phone image rarely supplies enough information to establish what occupied the sky. When an insect, reflection or focus error fits the evidence, identification weakens the extraordinary claim while strengthening understanding of how the report arose.

## A practical credibility test

Readers assessing a supposed UFO photograph can ask four questions before drawing conclusions.

**Was the object seen at the time?** An image-only discovery is particularly vulnerable to insects, birds, reflections and processing artefacts.

**Is the original available?** A screenshot or enlarged crop may be useful for discussion, but it is poor material for measurements or authentication.

**Does the shape remain stable?** Large changes between frames suggest motion blur, focus changes, camera processing or a nearby object rather than a rigid distant craft.

**Can its distance be established?** Without range or scale, apparent speed and size are guesses. A tiny fast object near the camera and a large slow object far away can produce similar images.

An [unresolved]({{ 'unresolved-843c8a/' | relative_url }}) photograph is not necessarily evidence of an exotic craft. Frequently it means that the file is too degraded, the metadata has been lost or the scene was not documented well enough to choose between several ordinary possibilities. The most defensible conclusion may therefore be “insufficient information”, rather than either confident debunking or confident claims of something extraordinary.

## The image can be stranger than the event

Camera artefacts reverse the usual assumption about UFO photography. The photograph is often expected to clarify what a witness saw, yet it can introduce shapes, edges and apparent motion that were never visible in the sky. Nearby insects can become discs or rods; reflections can become luminous orbs; defocused lights can inherit the shape of the aperture; and compression can manufacture apparently regular detail.

That pattern is central to understanding photographic reports investigated from North Rhine-Westphalia. The important distinction is not between “real photograph” and “fake photograph”. A completely genuine, unedited file can still give a deeply misleading impression of the object that produced it. The strongest investigations therefore examine the camera as carefully as the claimed UFO—and sometimes find that the most unusual feature of the case existed only inside the image.
