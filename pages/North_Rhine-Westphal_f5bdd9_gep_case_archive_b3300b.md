## Introduction

The GEP case archive records a process, not a catalogue of proven mysteries. A report enters the system because a witness could not identify an object or experience at the time. Investigators then preserve the basic circumstances, compare the account with ordinary causes and assign a classification reflecting both the proposed explanation and the quality of the remaining evidence. A case may therefore end as an identified aircraft, satellite, balloon or planet; as a report with insufficient data; or, much less often, as an [unresolved]({{ 'unresolved-843c8a/' | relative_url }}) case with varying degrees of apparent anomaly.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/North_Rhine-Westphal_f5bdd9_gep_case_archive_b3300b-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Case Archive" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For North Rhine-Westphalia, the archive matters because the Gesellschaft zur Erforschung des UFO-Phänomens, or GEP, is based in Lüdenscheid and has collected reports since 1972. Its files provide one of the clearest long-term records of how apparently unusual sightings in the state are reduced, qualified or left open. They also demonstrate an essential distinction: “unresolved” means that investigators could not establish a satisfactory explanation from the available material. It does not mean that an extraordinary craft was proved to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/ufo-sichtung-melden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

## What information each case contains

The publicly deposited GEP dataset is organised around individual reports. Its fields include a case number, observation date, country, federal state, postcode, location, local time, time zone, reporting date, method of first contact, a free-text account, preliminary sighting type, final classification, verification strength and proposed identification. Personal details are removed for privacy. The February 2026 release covers reports from 1972 to January 2026 and contains 6,404 records in total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073/latest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on zenodo.org.</span></span></span>

This structure allows a reader to follow a report from basic claim to investigative outcome. A typical entry might state that a witness saw several lights moving in a line, give the time and viewing direction, and then record an identification such as [Starlink satellites]({{ 'satellites-a19e68/' | relative_url }}). Another may describe a bright stationary light that looked triangular or pulsating through a phone camera, followed by an identification as Venus or another astronomical object. Entries can also remain partly blank when the report has only recently arrived, the witness supplied little detail or the investigation has not been completed.

For North Rhine-Westphalia, the February 2026 dataset contains 1,437 records. That figure should not be treated as the number of extraordinary events in the state. It includes explained reports, incomplete submissions, old cases entered retrospectively, photographs on which an object was noticed only afterwards, special files and reports still lacking a final judgement. It measures what reached the archive, not what physically occurred in the sky.

Several details make the archive more useful than a simple sightings map:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Observation and reporting dates are separate.** A witness may report an event immediately or decades later. Long delays normally make independent checking more difficult.
* **The original account is preserved in summary form.** This shows what the witness believed was unusual before the explanation was assigned.
* **Location data permit regional comparison.** Reports from Cologne, Duisburg, Wuppertal, Hamm and smaller towns can be examined alongside state-wide waves caused by the same satellite launch, meteor or lighting display.
* **The proposed stimulus is recorded.** “Identified” is not a single category: the archive distinguishes aircraft, planets, satellites, balloons, meteors, lighting effects, lens reflections and many other causes.
* **The classification and identification fields serve different purposes.** One indicates the evidential verdict; the other names the suspected or confirmed cause.

</div>

The online reporting form gathers the basic circumstances first, after which an investigator may contact the witness for clarification. The GEP says it also accepts reports passed on by institutions or made by professionals such as pilots and police officers. Photographs and testimony can therefore lead to further questions rather than being treated as self-explanatory evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/ufo-sichtung-melden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

## How verdicts are assigned

The GEP uses more than one classification system because the shape of a report and the result of an investigation are not the same thing. A Hynek-style label describes the reported encounter before its cause has been established. For example, a distant light, daylight object or claimed close encounter can be sorted by observation type. The GEP explicitly notes that this preliminary scheme is not designed to express the final investigative result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/forschung/ufo-klassifikationen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

The later Hendry-style judgement is more important when reading the archive’s verdicts.

**IFO**, meaning an identified flying object or identified phenomenon, is used when investigators consider an ordinary explanation sufficient. This does not necessarily mean the witness behaved irrationally. Aircraft seen head-on, bright planets in unstable air, distant balloons and recently introduced satellite formations can all look unfamiliar under particular viewing conditions.

**Near IFO** is used where the case resembles an ordinary stimulus but cannot be matched with complete certainty. It preserves a small degree of doubt without elevating the report into a strongly anomalous case.

**Insufficient data** means that the material does not support a dependable judgement. Important information may be missing: exact time, direction, duration, weather, original image file, witness availability or a sufficiently detailed description. This category is not halfway between an ordinary object and an extraordinary one. It is a statement about evidential weakness.

**Problematic UFO** is reserved for reports containing significant unusual features for which no clear conventional explanation has been established, although an ordinary cause cannot be excluded.

**Good UFO** denotes a more strongly unresolved report in which investigators regard the unusual features as difficult to reconcile with familiar objects. The GEP has published separate analyses of this small residual group, indicating that it treats these files as subjects for further study rather than as automatic proof of extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/forschung/ufo-klassifikationen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

A further verification code may indicate how securely an identification was established. An explanation supported by a matching flight, astronomical position or satellite passage is stronger than one based mainly on resemblance. This matters because two entries carrying the broad verdict “IFO” need not have equal evidential weight.

The practical investigation can include follow-up interviews, examination of photographs or video, internet research and enquiries to aviation or other relevant bodies. ## What the North Rhine-Westphalia records show

A direct analysis of the 1,437 North Rhine-Westphalia entries in the February 2026 dataset shows that 999 are classified as IFO. That is about 70 per cent of every state entry, including records that are still blank or incomplete, and approximately 83 per cent of entries with a recorded Hendry-style verdict. A further 49 are Near IFO, while 116 are labelled as having insufficient data. Only 16 are classified as Problematic UFO and nine as Good UFO. Two are marked as hoaxes; six are special cases outside the ordinary sequence. These totals describe the dataset as published and may change as files are reviewed or updated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073/latest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on zenodo.org.</span></span></span>

The identification fields show what drives the large explained majority. Among North Rhine-Westphalia entries, prominent groups include model hot-air balloons or sky lanterns, aircraft and helicopters, ordinary balloons, satellites, planets and stars, lighting equipment, camera artefacts, birds, insects and [drones]({{ 'drones-d4ad56/' | relative_url }}). The prominence of particular causes changes over time. Sky lanterns became a major source of reports during years when they were widely released; Starlink formations later produced a distinctive stream of “lights in a row” cases.

Individual NRW entries demonstrate how mundane causes can generate vivid descriptions. In November 2023, witnesses in Eschweiler and Duisburg reported strings of bright points moving across the sky; the archive identifies them as Starlink satellites. A Werdohl witness filmed a bright stationary object that appeared triangular and surrounded by an “energy field” on a phone screen; it was classified as Venus. In Wuppertal, a fast-moving milky light seen on clouds during a local power cut was attributed to a lighting-effects device. A group of lights filmed from two positions in Hamm was identified as illuminated helium balloons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10547073/files/UFO-UAP-Falldaten_GEP_1972-2023.csv?download=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO UAP Falldaten GEP 1972 2023.csv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO UAP Falldaten GEP 1972 2023.csv</span></span></span>

These cases are useful because the witness descriptions are not obviously absurd. A phone’s digital zoom can turn a point-like star or planet into a shifting geometric form. A spotlight projected onto cloud can appear to move at impossible speed because the visible patch is not a solid object. A satellite train can resemble a coordinated formation because the satellites share the same orbit. The explanation becomes persuasive only when timing, direction, appearance and known activity agree.

The records also expose a reporting bias. People rarely submit ordinary-looking aircraft or planets. They report the occasions on which those objects looked unusual. The archive is therefore a collection of difficult viewing circumstances and human uncertainty, not a neutral census of everything present in the sky.

## What unresolved status does not mean

The most common mistake is to read “unresolved” as a positive finding. In the archive, it is primarily the absence of a satisfactory identification. That absence may result from a genuinely unusual account, but it can also result from delay, poor observation conditions, missing files or a description too vague to test.

The distinction between insufficient data and the stronger unresolved categories is therefore crucial. An insufficient-data case cannot be used reliably to argue either for or against an extraordinary interpretation. It lacks the information needed to discriminate between alternatives. A Good UFO case carries greater residual interest because it has survived more scrutiny, but even that verdict does not identify the cause. It says that the case remains difficult under the GEP’s criteria.

Several limitations should shape any statistical reading of the archive.

First, the reports are self-selected. The dataset does not include every unusual sighting in North Rhine-Westphalia, only those sent to the GEP or later incorporated into its records. Publicity, internet access and awareness of the organisation influence who reports.

Second, cases differ sharply in quality. A contemporaneous observation with original images, several independent witnesses and exact timing is not equivalent to a childhood memory submitted 30 years later. Counting both as one report conceals that difference.

Third, blank fields are meaningful. In the 2026 release, hundreds of North Rhine-Westphalia records have no Hendry verdict. Some are minimally documented, historical, newly received or not suited to ordinary classification. Including them in a simple “percentage unexplained” calculation can produce misleading results.

Fourth, classifications are investigative judgements rather than official state determinations. The GEP is a civilian citizen-science organisation, not a police, military or aviation authority. Its published work is valuable because it preserves reasoning and proposed causes, but verdicts remain open to criticism and revision. The organisation’s own research literature stresses individual case study, data management and clearer definitions as necessary conditions for progress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10579210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on zenodo.org.</span></span></span>

Finally, the archive records summaries rather than every item in the underlying file. A short public entry may not include interview transcripts, original media, correspondence or the detailed reasoning presented in the GEP’s journal. Members receive broader database access, including additional case details and images, while investigated reports are also discussed in the organisation’s periodical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-forschung.de/mitgliedschaft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-forschung.de">[UFO Forschung]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-forschung.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufo-forschung.de.</span></span></span>

## The archive’s strongest verdict

Taken as a whole, the GEP archive weakens the idea that a high number of reports represents a high number of extraordinary craft. North Rhine-Westphalia contributes many cases because it is populous, urbanised and closely watched, and because the principal German civilian reporting centre is located there. Once reports are examined, ordinary objects dominate the classified record.

The small unresolved remainder should not be discarded, but it must be described accurately. The archive supports three different conclusions: many witnesses encounter real but misidentified objects; some reports cannot be judged because the evidence is too poor; and a much smaller group retains unusual features after investigation. None of those conclusions requires the others to be ignored.

That layered outcome is the archive’s real value to North Rhine-Westphalia’s UFO history. It replaces the crude choice between “all nonsense” and “alien spacecraft” with a documented chain of observation, testing and graded uncertainty. Its verdicts are most informative not when they supply a dramatic final answer, but when they show exactly how far the available evidence permits an investigator to go.
