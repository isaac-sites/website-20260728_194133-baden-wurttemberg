## Introduction

The famous Nuremberg “sky battle” was not recorded as an encounter with alien spacecraft. It comes from an authentic illustrated broadsheet issued by the printer Hans Glaser in 1561, describing alarming shapes around the rising Sun and presenting them as a divine warning. The modern UFO interpretation appeared much later, after readers began treating the woodcut’s spheres, rods and tubes as pictures of machines rather than elements of sixteenth-century religious and visual culture.

That distinction matters to [Bavaria]({{ 'bavarias-ufo-stories-under-the-evidence/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history. The document itself is genuine and striking, but it is not an official report, a collection of witness statements or a technical drawing made to modern evidential standards. Atmospheric optics may explain at least part of the display, yet the surviving evidence is too stylised and incomplete for a confident reconstruction. Nuremberg 1561 is therefore best understood as a historical sky report that acquired a new identity in the flying-saucer age—not as a proven ancient UFO battle.

## What Hans Glaser’s broadsheet actually depicts

The surviving sheet is held by the Zentralbibliothek Zürich and comes from the collection assembled by the Zurich clergyman Johann Jakob Wick. Its catalogue describes a single coloured woodcut and printed text, measuring about 26 by 38 centimetres and produced in Nuremberg in 1561. The archive identifies it as a depiction of a celestial phenomenon whose accompanying text relates the display to divine power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.e-manuscripta.ch/zuzneb/content/titleinfo/2723852" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-manuscripta.ch">[E-Manuscripta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-manuscripta.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Himmelserscheinung über Nürnberg vom 14</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 1561December 31, 1560 — Glaser, Hans: [Himmelserscheinung über Nürnberg vom 14. April 1561]. Bey Hanns Glaser Brieffmaler, Bey Hann...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1560</span></span></span>

Glaser placed the event at daybreak on 14 April 1561. His account says that many people in and around Nuremberg saw a frightening apparition in or around the Sun. The print shows a human-faced Sun surrounded by red arcs, numerous coloured balls, crosses, long rods and several large tube-like forms. Beneath them is a broad black spear- or wedge-shaped object. At the right edge, coloured spheres appear to descend beyond the city amid clouds of smoke. The original image confirms how dense and theatrical the composition is: it presents the whole sky as an ordered field of symbolic shapes rather than as a restrained observational diagram.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.e-manuscripta.ch/zuzneb/download/pdf/2723852" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-manuscripta.ch">[E-Manuscripta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-manuscripta.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-Manuscripta...</span></span></span>

Glaser described the objects as moving against one another for about an hour, with some apparently falling towards the ground before the rest disappeared. This language created the basis for the later phrase “sky battle”. Yet his conclusion was explicitly religious. The spectacle was treated as a sign from God and as a warning that readers should repent rather than continue ignoring earlier wonders. The purpose of the sheet was therefore not simply to say what had been seen. It also told the audience what the sight meant and how people should respond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/celestial-wonders-in-reformation-germany/introduction-celestial-wonders-confessional-conflicts-and-apocalypticism/A6C56C906F983D6A010A4AD001B8BB96" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">According to Michaela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressIntroduction: Celestial Wonders, Confessional Conflicts and ...In sixteenth-century Germany, Wunderzeichen (&#x27;wo...</span></span></span>

This is crucial when assessing its evidential value. Glaser may have drawn upon genuine observation, local testimony, existing symbolic conventions or a mixture of all three. The sheet does not identify individual witnesses, record their separate positions, distinguish first-hand evidence from hearsay or provide measurable information such as angles, distances, weather conditions and directions of movement. Even the claim that “many men and women” saw the display comes through Glaser’s publication rather than through surviving independent testimonies.

The word “broadsheet” can also mislead modern readers. It was not a newspaper report in the present-day sense. Early printed sheets of this kind combined current events, spectacle, moral instruction and religious interpretation. They were designed to be viewed, read aloud, discussed and remembered. Their dramatic imagery could communicate the seriousness of a message to people who would not necessarily study every line of text. Cambridge historian Ken Kurihara places such “wonder-signs” within the religious conflicts and apocalyptic expectations of Reformation Germany, where unusual natural events were routinely interpreted as meaningful interventions in human affairs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/celestial-wonders-in-reformation-germany/introduction-celestial-wonders-confessional-conflicts-and-apocalypticism/A6C56C906F983D6A010A4AD001B8BB96" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">According to Michaela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressIntroduction: Celestial Wonders, Confessional Conflicts and ...In sixteenth-century Germany, Wunderzeichen (&#x27;wo...</span></span></span>

Glaser was also not producing a one-off scientific record outside his normal trade. The Zurich collection contains other celestial-wonder sheets attributed to him, including reports of an unusual solar appearance and a remarkable lunar display seen in Nuremberg during the same broad period. That wider output makes the 1561 sheet look less like an isolated attempt to document unprecedented technology and more like one example of a popular printed genre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.e-manuscripta.ch/bilder/nav/classification/45?facets=d100%3D%221500%22+and+collections%3D%221726382%22+and+name.aut%3D%22Glaser%2C+Hans%22&amp;max=50&amp;o=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-manuscripta.ch">[E-Manuscripta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-manuscripta.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on e-manuscripta.ch.</span></span></span>

## How a religious omen became alien machinery

The modern UFO reading depends heavily on visual resemblance. To a twentieth- or twenty-first-century viewer, the long cylinders can resemble launch tubes or carrier craft; the balls may look like metallic vehicles; the black wedge can suggest a triangular aircraft; and the smoke below can be read as evidence of crashes. Once that technological vocabulary is imposed on the image, the entire scene appears to tell a familiar science-fiction story.

That reading reverses the original process. Glaser’s audience was encouraged to understand the display through religious and military imagery already familiar to them: crosses, lances, cannon-like forms, blood-red colour and conflict in the heavens. Modern UFO advocates often treat these shapes as imperfect attempts by pre-industrial witnesses to describe advanced machines. An equally plausible historical reading is that unfamiliar light in the sky was translated into the symbolic forms that a sixteenth-century printer and audience knew best.

The print’s later fame owes much to the psychologist Carl Jung. In *Flying Saucers: A Modern Myth of Things Seen in the Skies*, first published in 1958, Jung reproduced the Nuremberg broadsheet while discussing the recurring symbolic forms attached to unusual aerial reports. His central concern was the psychological and myth-making dimension of flying saucers, not a claim that Glaser had documented extraterrestrial warfare. The Nuremberg image nevertheless entered modern UFO culture through a book devoted to flying saucers, making it easier for later writers to detach the picture from its original sermon-like conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/luminist/EB/I-J-K/Jung%20-%20Flying%20Saucers.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com">[Wasabi Technologies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jung Flying Saucers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jung Flying Saucers</span></span></span>

This twentieth-century reframing produced several persistent distortions.

**“Hundreds of witnesses left detailed accounts.”** Glaser asserted that many people saw the phenomenon, but no known archive of separately recorded witness testimony supports the elaborate narratives sometimes repeated online.

**“The print shows a crash site.”** It shows smoke and descending forms outside the city, but there is no surviving record of recovered objects, impact debris, casualties or an investigation of physical remains.

**“The cylinders are motherships releasing smaller craft.”** That is an interpretation of the drawing, not wording established by a technical observer. The same forms can be read as stylised rays, cannon-like symbolic objects or visual devices used to organise a crowded composition.

**“The black object was a triangular spacecraft.”** Glaser’s text compares it to a large black spear. Descriptions such as “triangle”, “arrowhead” and “mothership” belong largely to modern retellings.

**“The broadsheet is an eyewitness photograph in another medium.”** Woodcuts were constructed images. Their scale, positioning and symbolism were chosen by the maker and could compress different phases of an event into a single scene.

Later retellings also tend to remove the religious ending because it does not fit the alien-battle narrative. Yet that ending is not an incidental addition. It reveals what Glaser believed the publication was for. Removing it converts a moral warning into a supposedly neutral sighting report and makes the modern interpretation appear much stronger than the source itself allows.

A comparable pattern can be seen in the Basel celestial broadsheet of 1566, which described dark spheres and dramatic coloured skies but framed them as warnings demanding moral reform. The similarity does not prove that both cases had the same physical cause. It does show that reports of conflict-like celestial signs belonged to a recognisable early modern tradition rather than forming an isolated record of mechanical craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2024/07/the-celestial-event-over-basel-in-1566/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nationalmuseum.ch">[Swiss National Museum Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nationalmuseum.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the celestial event over basel in 1566</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the celestial event over basel in 1566</span></span></span>

## Atmospheric explanations and the limits of reconstruction

The most common natural explanation is a complex solar-halo display. Halos form when sunlight is refracted or reflected by ice crystals in the atmosphere. When the Sun is low, these displays can include bright patches beside it, arcs, vertical columns and crossing bands. Ordinary “sun dogs” are only one part of this larger family of optical effects. Several simultaneous halo features could therefore produce a sky filled with repeated lights and geometric structures, especially near sunrise.

Certain features of Glaser’s scene are compatible with this possibility. The event is centred on the rising Sun; red arcs surround it; repeated spots appear on either side; and long bands cross the sky. Bright halo fragments can change in intensity as cloud layers move, giving stationary optical features an apparent life cycle. Thin clouds, changing illumination and strong colour near the horizon could also have made the scene seem unstable or combative.

Historical records must nevertheless be handled cautiously when used for meteorological reconstruction. Research on other early celestial drawings shows that images may be copied, altered, stylised or shaped by the symbolic expectations of their period. Establishing provenance and separating observation from later visual interpretation are essential before attempting a precise physical model.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04192" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

A halo explanation therefore remains plausible rather than proven. Glaser did not record the angular positions needed to match individual shapes to named optical features. The woodcut may combine several stages of the display, exaggerate the number of objects or include imagery introduced for moral effect. The falling spheres and smoke do not correspond neatly to a standard halo formation, although they may represent clouds, distant weather, metaphorical defeat or a separate phenomenon folded into the story.

Other natural suggestions—birds, meteor activity, unusual clouds or an auroral display—fit parts of the narrative less well. Birds can create moving formations but do not readily account for a structured solar display. Meteors are brief and generally do not remain concentrated around the Sun for an hour. Auroras are normally easier to see in darkness and would be difficult to reconcile with the report’s strong focus on sunrise. A mixed event involving atmospheric optics, cloud effects and interpretive embellishment may be more realistic than any single simple cause.

The date itself does not allow the sky to be recreated with scientific confidence. Modern astronomy can calculate the Sun’s position, but it cannot recover the precise ice-crystal population, cloud layers, visibility, local weather or sequence of impressions experienced by observers. Nor can it determine how much of the woodcut was based on what Glaser personally saw. Any explanation that claims to account for every sphere, tube and cross risks treating symbolic artwork as if it were calibrated data.

The correct evidential judgement is therefore narrower than either extreme. The print cannot prove an alien battle, but sceptics cannot use one proposed halo diagram to demonstrate exactly what every reported feature was. The case is weak as a modern UFO report because the source does not contain the information needed to test extraordinary claims. Its unresolved elements survive because the record is sparse, not because the extraterrestrial interpretation has positive physical evidence.

## What the Nuremberg case contributes to Bavaria’s UFO history

Nuremberg 1561 matters because it shows how a UFO case can be created retrospectively. The underlying document was not forged, and there may well have been an impressive sight in the Bavarian sky. The myth developed when the authentic source was removed from its original genre and interpreted through twentieth-century ideas about spacecraft, aerial fleets and crashed vehicles.

That makes the incident different from Bavaria’s later aviation-era reports. Modern UFO cases may involve pilots, radar operators, military reporting chains, photographs or timed observations. Glaser’s broadsheet offers none of those forms of evidence. It belongs to the history of how people represented unusual skies, not to the history of modern aerospace investigation.

Its strongest lessons are methodological:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* An authentic artefact can support an inauthentic interpretation.
* A claim of multiple witnesses is not equivalent to preserved multiple testimony.
* A vivid image is not automatically an observationally accurate image.
* The original cultural meaning of a source matters as much as the shapes shown in it.
* “Unexplained” can mean that the evidence is too poor to reconstruct the event, not that an extraordinary cause has survived testing.

</div>

Later publicity strengthened awareness of the broadsheet but did not strengthen the alien claim. No new witness archive, physical trace or contemporary investigation has emerged to convert it into evidence of machinery in the sky. Digital access provided by the Zentralbibliothek Zürich has instead made the original object easier to inspect, including its religious framing and its status as one sheet within a much wider culture of printed celestial warnings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.e-manuscripta.ch/zuzneb/content/titleinfo/2723852" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-manuscripta.ch">[E-Manuscripta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-manuscripta.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Himmelserscheinung über Nürnberg vom 14</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 1561December 31, 1560 — Glaser, Hans: [Himmelserscheinung über Nürnberg vom 14. The most balanced classification is therefore **historically genuine, physically uncertain and ufologically weak**. Something may have been observed over Nuremberg at sunrise in April 1561, and atmospheric optics offer a credible broad explanation. But the famous aerial battle is chiefly a story produced by the interaction of a sixteenth-century omen sheet with the imagery of the modern flying-saucer age.
