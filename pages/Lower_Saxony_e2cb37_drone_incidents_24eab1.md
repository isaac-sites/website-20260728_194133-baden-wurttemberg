## Introduction

In [Lower Saxony]({{ 'when-strange-lights-crossed-lower/' | relative_url }}), drones are increasingly occupying the cultural and investigative space once filled by UFO reports. Police recorded 41 suspected drone incidents or sightings of unusual position lights in 2022, 70 in 2023 and 131 in 2024. The figure then rose to 435 in 2025. Yet this is not a count of 435 confirmed aircraft: the official category includes objects that could not be reliably identified and, in some cases, amounted only to lights in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lower_Saxony_e2cb37_drone_incidents_24eab1-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Drones" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction is central to understanding the phenomenon. Some incidents near military bases, ports, energy facilities and airports appear serious enough to raise concerns about espionage or sabotage. Others may involve careless hobby pilots, legitimate aircraft, distant lights or reports too incomplete to classify. The modern question is therefore not simply whether “UFOs” have become drones. It is whether better technology and a changed security climate have replaced one uncertain label with another.

## The rise in suspected drone incidents

The official figures show a clear rise, but they require careful reading. A November 2025 answer from the Lower Saxony government counted 592 police-recorded incidents between January 2022 and 17 November 2025. The annual totals were 41, 70, 131 and 350 respectively. A later government answer, covering all of 2025, raised that year’s figure to 435. Both documents use wording that combines suspected drone overflights with observations of conspicuous position lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

The geographical distribution was uneven. By 17 November 2025, the Oldenburg police region had recorded 132 incidents that year and Lüneburg 82, compared with 59 in Hannover, 43 in Osnabrück, 18 in Göttingen and 16 in Braunschweig. The prominence of Oldenburg and Lüneburg matters because northern Lower Saxony contains ports, military sites, energy installations, coastal approaches and large areas of relatively dark sky. It does not prove that these districts were being systematically targeted, but it does show that the increase was not merely a Hanover airport story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

Several different forces probably contributed to the rise. Consumer and commercial drones have become more common, increasing both the number of genuine flights and public familiarity with their appearance. At the same time, Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine sharpened official concern about reconnaissance, sabotage and so-called hybrid activity below the threshold of conventional warfare. Greater awareness among police, infrastructure operators and the public can itself produce more reporting, including reports that would previously have been filed as unexplained lights or not reported at all. Lower Saxony’s domestic intelligence service now explicitly treats recurring drone sightings over strategically important facilities as part of the wider hybrid-threat picture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mi.niedersachsen.de/download/229413/Verfassungsschutzbericht_Niedersachsen_2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mi.niedersachsen.de">[Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mi.niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Verfassungsschutzbericht Niedersachsen 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Niedersachsen...</span></span></span>

The figures therefore demonstrate a growth in **reported suspicious observations**, not a proven four-year surge in hostile drones. That may sound like a technical qualification, but it is the main fact separating evidence-led analysis from alarmism.

## Reports near sensitive locations

The most striking Lower Saxony cluster came in the final week of February 2025. Investigative reporting by NDR, WDR and the *Süddeutsche Zeitung* described suspicious objects near the Nordholz military airfield outside Cuxhaven, the [Wilhelmshaven]({{ 'wilhelmshaven/' | relative_url }}) naval base, the Jemgum natural-gas storage facility in East Frisia and locations around Cuxhaven and Bremerhaven. The objects were reportedly seen at night, sometimes circling briefly before disappearing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/ndr-wdr/drohnen-ueberfluege-102.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tagesschau.de">[tagesschau.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span></span></span>

Sources cited in that investigation described wingspans of roughly three to six metres, which would place the reported objects well beyond the familiar small quadcopters sold to hobbyists. The Bundeswehr confirmed that sightings had occurred within its area of responsibility, while the Lower Saxony interior ministry declined to discuss operational details. No aircraft was intercepted, its launch site was not established publicly and no operator was identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/ndr-wdr/drohnen-ueberfluege-102.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tagesschau.de">[tagesschau.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span></span></span>

The coastal geography encouraged speculation that some drones might have approached from the North Sea or been launched from ships. German security sources had already considered similar possibilities after repeated overflights around the Brunsbüttel industrial and energy complex in neighbouring Schleswig-Holstein in August 2024. However, the northern cluster did not produce public proof that Russia, a particular ship or any named intelligence service was responsible. The reporting distinguished between a suspicion considered plausible by security officials and an attribution established by evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/ndr-wdr/drohnen-ueberfluege-102.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tagesschau.de">[tagesschau.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ballung von neuen Drohnensichtungen im Norden &#124; tagesschau.de</span></span></span>

Lower Saxony’s 2025 intelligence report goes further in its general assessment. It says drones can photograph areas not visible from public roads, document patterns of movement and collect more current, detailed information than ordinary commercial satellite imagery. It assesses that at least some overflights of critical infrastructure and military property may serve espionage, sabotage preparation, testing of defensive responses, provocation or deliberate public intimidation. At the same time, it acknowledges individual pilot misconduct as another cause and says that the models successfully identified have generally been conventionally available aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mi.niedersachsen.de/download/229413/Verfassungsschutzbericht_Niedersachsen_2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mi.niedersachsen.de">[Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mi.niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Verfassungsschutzbericht Niedersachsen 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Niedersachsen...</span></span></span>

That combination is important. Official concern is not invented, but neither does the intelligence report claim that every unidentified light near a sensitive site is a hostile reconnaissance platform.

## Airports provide the clearest immediate risk

Drone reports at airports differ from sightings over an industrial facility because the danger does not depend on hostile intent. A small drone entering an approach path can force controllers to suspend movements even when it is being flown by an irresponsible hobbyist.

By November 2025, the Lower Saxony government said suspected drone incidents had caused two restrictions to flight operations in the state during that year, compared with none in 2024. German air-traffic-control data separately showed four drone-related disruptions at Hannover Airport by the end of August 2025. Reports normally came from pilots during take-off or landing or from observers in the control tower.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

A concrete example followed on 5 November. Hannover Airport suspended operations for about 45 minutes after an approaching pilot reported a drone. Three aircraft were diverted and other services were delayed. The object was reportedly above a nearby industrial area rather than directly over a runway, but its position was close enough to the flight path for the airport, federal police and air-traffic control to treat it as a collision risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.welt.de/regionales/niedersachsen/article690bd9360580923d09988b1a/nach-drohnensichtung-sperrung-von-airport-hannover-beendet.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: welt.de">[DIE WELT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">welt.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DIE WELTDrohnensichtung: Flughafen Hannover 45 Minuten gesperrt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eine der umgeleiteten Maschinen sollte nach dpa-Informationen aus Frankfurt nach Hannover fliegen und wurde nach Hamburg umgeleitet...</span></span></span>

These airport cases are among the strongest examples of confirmed consequences arising from an uncertain observation. Authorities did not need to establish the operator’s motive, model or nationality before acting. The safety threshold is deliberately lower: an unverified object in the wrong place can be operationally significant even when the underlying report remains unresolved.

For UFO history, this creates an instructive reversal. Older unexplained-aircraft stories often gained importance because [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses-6320a8/' | relative_url }}) claimed extraordinary speed or manoeuvres. Modern drone incidents can matter precisely because nothing extraordinary is observed. A slow light near an approach path may be more consequential than a spectacular distant object that poses no physical danger.

## Why confirmation remains difficult

Identifying a small aircraft at night is harder than the word “drone sighting” suggests. A witness may see only one or more lights without a visible body, making it difficult to judge size, range, direction or speed. A nearby consumer quadcopter and a much larger fixed-wing vehicle farther away can produce similar visual impressions. Aircraft seen head-on, stars through moving cloud, helicopters and illuminated balloons can also be mistaken for drones when no sound, imagery or radar track is available.

The problem is visible in the official terminology. Lower Saxony repeatedly describes incidents as involving either drones **or conspicuous position lights**, and states that the aircraft type could not be confirmed in every case. In its 2025 intelligence report, the state added that most reported drone types remained unknown or could not be established reliably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Landtag Niedersachsen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

No single detection method resolves every ambiguity. Radio-frequency sensors can recognise control links from many conventional drones and may help locate the operator, but they are less useful when an aircraft flies autonomously, uses unfamiliar signals or communicates through another network. Radar can detect objects that emit no control signal, though small, slow targets must be distinguished from birds and environmental clutter. Optical and thermal cameras can provide visual confirmation but depend on line of sight, weather, background and sufficient resolution. Technical research therefore favours combining radar, radio and imaging rather than relying on eyewitnesses or one sensor alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/countering-drone-threats-to-shipping/countering-drone-threats-to-shipping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Countering drone threats to shipping</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">* Most RF (radio frequency) detection systems rely on the equipment having suitable library references or decryption protocols for the th...</span></span></span>

Lower Saxony has a locally relevant role in this technical response. The German Aerospace Center in Braunschweig operates a mobile radar-based detector designed to find, identify and track small, uncooperative unmanned aircraft. Its counter-drone research combines detection with technologies intended to intercept or neutralise unauthorised vehicles near airports, major events and critical infrastructure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dlr.de/en/ft/about-us/departments/unmanned-aircraft/infrastructure-of-the-department/mobile-drone-detector" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dlr.de">[DLR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dlr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dlr.de.</span></span></span>

Detection, however, is only the first stage. Authorities must still decide whether an object is legal, negligent, criminal or potentially hostile; identify who has jurisdiction; preserve evidence; and choose a response that does not create a greater hazard. Jamming can interfere with legitimate communications, while shooting at a drone over an airport, refinery or populated area risks uncontrolled debris. This helps explain why interception is much less common than observation and temporary airspace closure.

## From UFO file to security incident

The growth of drone reporting has not eliminated unidentified aerial phenomena in Lower Saxony. It has changed the language used to describe them. A generation ago, a witness reporting an unfamiliar moving light might have spoken of a UFO. Today, the first assumption is often that it was a drone, especially near an airport, naval site or energy installation.

That assumption is frequently reasonable. Drones are real, widely available and capable of hovering, changing direction sharply and operating after dark. They can also carry cameras and follow programmed routes, giving apparently purposeful behaviour to lights that would once have seemed mysterious.

But “drone” can function as a premature explanation as easily as “UFO” once did. The state’s own database demonstrates this problem by combining verified and unverified observations. A report may be classified administratively as a suspected drone incident because that is the relevant safety or policing category, even though investigators never obtain a photograph, radar return, recovered aircraft or identified pilot.

A useful evidence scale is therefore:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Confirmed drone:** supported by clear imagery, sensor data, recovery, remote identification or an identified operator.
* **Probable drone:** behaviour and context fit an unmanned aircraft, but decisive identification is missing.
* **Unidentified light or aircraft:** insufficient information to distinguish a drone from another airborne or astronomical source.
* **Security attribution:** evidence connects the flight to espionage, sabotage preparation or another organised purpose.

</div>

Most public discussion collapses these levels. A light becomes a drone; a drone near infrastructure becomes suspicious; and “suspicious” becomes evidence of a foreign operation. Lower Saxony’s official records support concern at the first two stages but rarely provide public evidence sufficient for the fourth.

## The state’s developing response

Lower Saxony’s government regards unauthorised drone use as a security-relevant phenomenon, especially around critical infrastructure and military property. Its stated objectives are earlier detection, evidence-grade documentation, effective intervention and better information for infrastructure operators. Planned investment for 2026 exceeded €7 million for detection and counter-drone equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

The state has also withheld detailed location data. In its answer to the Landtag, the government argued that publishing a close geographical breakdown would effectively identify which facilities are considered especially sensitive and could help a hostile actor plan future flights. This limits independent scrutiny, but the reasoning is not trivial: a complete public incident map could become a guide to security priorities and blind spots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landtag-niedersachsen.de/drucksachen/drucksachen_19_10000/09001-09500/19-09172.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landtag-niedersachsen.de">[Landtag Niedersachsen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landtag-niedersachsen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Landtag Niedersachsen</span></span></span>

There is nevertheless a governance tension. Secrecy protects facilities, while transparency is needed to distinguish confirmed incursions from mistaken reports and to assess whether expensive countermeasures are proportionate. The present public record gives annual totals and broad categories but little information about how many cases were verified, how many were explained, how many involved sensor corroboration and how many remained simple eyewitness reports.

That missing denominator is the largest weakness in claims of a drone “wave”. The increase is real as a policing workload and as a sign of public concern. The threat is credible because drones can collect intelligence, endanger aircraft and probe sensitive sites. Yet the available figures do not show how much of the rise represents hostile activity, ordinary rule-breaking, improved reporting or lights that were never drones at all.

## Are drones replacing UFOs?

In one sense, yes. Drones now offer a familiar technological explanation for hovering lights, unusual night-time movement and repeated observations around airports or military property. They have shifted unexplained-sky reporting away from speculative extraterrestrial narratives and towards aviation safety, criminal law and national security.

In another sense, the underlying UFO problem remains unchanged: observers still struggle to estimate distance and size, records remain incomplete, and an unresolved report is often asked to carry more meaning than the evidence permits. “Suspected drone” is more plausible than “alien spacecraft”, but it is not automatically a confirmed identification.

The strongest conclusion from Lower Saxony’s recent records is therefore neither that the state is facing hundreds of foreign spy drones nor that the incidents are merely public panic. It is that a genuine new category of aerial risk has become mixed with the older category of uncertain lights. Separating the two requires timed reports, precise location and direction data, photographs, radar or radio detection, and evidence linking an aircraft to an operator. Until that evidence exists, many of the state’s modern drone reports remain what earlier generations would simply have called unidentified flying objects.
