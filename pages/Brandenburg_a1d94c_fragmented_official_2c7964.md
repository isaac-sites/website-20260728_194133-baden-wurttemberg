## Introduction

[Brandenburg]({{ 'why-brandenburgs-ufo-record-remains-so/' | relative_url }})’s official UFO record does not sit in a single government archive. A strange light reported near Potsdam might become a police incident log; an object seen by a pilot could enter an aviation-safety system; an unexplained radar contact might remain within military operational records; and a civilian account may survive only in a newspaper or the database of a voluntary investigation group. Germany has historically had no acknowledged federal civilian service that receives, investigates, classifies and publishes UFO reports as one continuous body of evidence.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Brandenburg_a1d94c_fragmented_official_2c7964-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Records" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That fragmentation matters because it changes what “no official files” really means. It does not show that nothing was reported, nor that every report was secretly investigated. It usually means that German institutions recorded events according to their ordinary responsibilities—public safety, air traffic, defence or intelligence—rather than under a common UFO heading. For Brandenburg, researchers therefore have to reconstruct the record across institutions, jurisdictions and archival systems.

## The federal government’s stated position

The clearest federal statement came in June 2008, when a member of the Bundestag asked how many UFO or extraterrestrial sightings the government had registered since 2000 and whether it would release its files, as Britain and France had done. Parliamentary State Secretary Peter Altmaier replied that the federal government possessed no information about such sightings in Germany and consequently had no UFO sighting files suitable for publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/16/095/1609554.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dserver.bundestag.de">[Bundestag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dserver.bundestag.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1609554...</span></span></span>

Read literally, the answer sounds comprehensive. In administrative terms, however, it was narrower than it first appeared. It did not establish that no police force, air-navigation body, military unit or intelligence office had ever recorded an unidentified object. Nor did it say that every federal ministry and subordinate agency had conducted a coordinated search across records filed under other descriptions.

A 2009 paper by the Bundestag’s Research Services made that limitation explicit. It concluded that Germany had never been legally obliged to document or investigate UFO reports under a United Nations initiative discussed in the late 1970s. It also observed that the government’s public position said nothing conclusive about whether German authorities had conducted relevant investigations in the past. The paper considered it plausible that unfamiliar aircraft, satellites or possible adversary technology could have attracted military attention during the Cold War without being treated as a civilian UFO programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/406336/741fdc9b7e96b9346e4e3414225b2835/wd-8-104-09-pdf-data.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundestag.de">[Deutscher Bundestag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundestag.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deutscher Bundestag</span></span></span>

The distinction is important. A government can truthfully say that it has no central collection of “UFO files” while still possessing records of unidentified radar returns, airspace infringements, unusual lights, suspected balloons or aircraft whose operators could not be established. Those records are organised around the authority’s task, not around the witness’s interpretation.

## Where a Brandenburg report may be recorded

No single route applies to every observation. The destination depends on whether the event appeared merely unusual or created a possible safety, criminal or defence concern.

**Police records.** A member of the public who sees lights, hears an unexplained aircraft or suspects dangerous activity may contact the Brandenburg police. The resulting record is likely to concern the reported circumstance—such as a disturbance, possible drone, flare, aircraft or suspicious object—rather than to become a permanent UFO case file. Routine reports may remain local, be closed after checks or eventually be deleted under applicable retention and data-protection rules. A brief police entry may therefore confirm that somebody called without preserving enough detail for a later scientific assessment.

**Civil aviation systems.** Reports involving pilots, controllers or risks to aircraft can enter aviation occurrence-reporting channels. European and German safety rules are designed to capture events that may affect aviation, including unknown traffic, objects or hazards. Their purpose is accident prevention, not determining whether a witness encountered an exotic craft. The same incident might consequently be classified as an airspace infringement, drone encounter, balloon, laser illumination or unidentified traffic rather than as a UFO.

A notable change came in 2025, when Germany’s Federal Aviation Office supported a reporting initiative run by the University of Würzburg’s Interdisciplinary Research Centre for Extraterrestrial Studies. Pilots were offered a route to submit unusual observations for scientific assessment, with plans to develop a database capable of receiving images and video. This provides a more recognisable UAP reporting path, but it is a university-led research arrangement supported by the aviation authority, not a German equivalent of a comprehensive national UFO bureau.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uni-wuerzburg.de/en/news-and-events/news/detail/news/uap-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uni-wuerzburg.de">[Universität Würzburg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uni-wuerzburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-wuerzburg.de.</span></span></span>

**Military and air-defence material.** An unknown radar contact or object near restricted airspace may be handled as an operational matter. Initial questions would concern identification, flight safety, intrusion or hostile technology. Any surviving paperwork could therefore be dispersed among unit logs, command records, intelligence assessments and defence-ministry files. Some material may be classified, while other records may eventually be transferred to the Federal Archives.

The Federal Archives’ Military Archives Department in Freiburg holds records from the Federal Ministry of Defence, the Bundeswehr and the former East German National People’s Army. Its holdings extend far beyond UFO claims and are arranged by the institutions that created them. Researchers normally need a date, unit, location or operational context; a simple keyword search for “UFO” is unlikely to recover every relevant document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundesarchiv.de/en/research-our-records/research-archive-material/research-on-persons-and-ancestors/personal-documents-of-military-provenance/special-areas-of-military-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundesarchiv.de">[Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundesarchiv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv Special Areas of Military Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stasi-Unterlagen-ArchivSpecial Areas of Military Documents - The Federal ArchivesAt our Freiburg location, we archive more than 50 kilome...</span></span></span>

**East German security records.** Brandenburg’s history also spans the former German Democratic Republic. Reports near [military sites]({{ 'military-sites/' | relative_url }}), borders, government installations or Soviet forces could have attracted police, military or state-security attention. Records of the former Ministry for State Security are now held within the Federal Archives’ dedicated Stasi Records Archive, while other East German civil and military holdings are distributed across separate archival departments. Their arrangement again reflects the original agency and function rather than a unified catalogue of aerial anomalies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundesarchiv.de/en/research-our-records/help/contact-person/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundesarchiv.de">[Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundesarchiv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv Contact person</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stasi-Unterlagen-ArchivContact person - The Federal ArchivesMonday - Thursday: 9. **State and local archives.** Files produced by Brandenburg authorities may eventually pass into state archival custody if they are selected for permanent preservation. Selection is not automatic: archives retain records judged historically or legally valuable, not every telephone call or routine operational note. Local authority papers, emergency-service logs and administrative correspondence may also remain at district or municipal level before transfer—or may not survive at all.

**Press and private databases.** When no official record can be located, local journalism and voluntary investigators often preserve the only accessible account. These sources can provide dates, witness descriptions and proposed explanations, but they do not have uniform standards. A newspaper report may compress or dramatise testimony, while private groups differ in how thoroughly they check astronomy, aircraft movements, satellite passes and weather conditions.

## Why France offers a revealing comparison

France demonstrates what Germany has lacked. GEIPAN, a department of the French space agency CNES, has a defined national mission to receive, analyse, investigate, archive and publish reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena. Cases pass through a stated process that includes creating a file, conducting an investigation, assessing the quality of the information, anonymising documents and publishing a classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

GEIPAN is not an air-defence alert service and does not investigate extraterrestrial visitation as a presumed explanation. Its practical value is administrative consistency: reports from different regions can enter one system, receive broadly comparable treatment and remain searchable after investigation. GEIPAN itself notes that, without this national structure, police officers and voluntary investigators would work locally and in a scattered fashion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Germany’s record is almost the inverse. Separate institutions may each perform sensible work within their remit, but there has historically been no shared public case number, common evidence standard or national classification linking the original report to the eventual explanation. This makes regional questions—such as whether Brandenburg experienced a genuine sighting cluster—much harder to answer. An apparent rise may reflect heavier press coverage, changes in public reporting habits or improved collection by private groups rather than a rise in unexplained events.

## The Bundestag paper was not a secret investigation

The release of the Bundestag Research Services paper has sometimes been presented as proof that parliament maintained a concealed UFO dossier. That interpretation overstates what the document was.

The paper was a background study prepared for parliamentary use. Its subject included extraterrestrial life, international policy and the United Nations decision concerning unidentified flying objects. It reviewed public statements and raised the possibility that German authorities could have examined unfamiliar phenomena for military reasons. It did not disclose a federal programme, a catalogue of Brandenburg incidents or evidence of recovered technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/406336/741fdc9b7e96b9346e4e3414225b2835/wd-8-104-09-pdf-data.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundestag.de">[Deutscher Bundestag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundestag.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deutscher Bundestag</span></span></span>

Its delayed publication arose from a legal dispute about access to papers produced by the Bundestag’s Research Services. After litigation over Germany’s freedom-of-information law, the study was made public in 2015. The dispute was therefore significant for transparency, but the fact that release required a court case does not transform the paper into an operational intelligence file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ezw-berlin.de/publikationen/artikel/ufo-gutachten-des-deutschen-bundestags-veroeffentlicht/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ezw-berlin.de">[ezw-berlin.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ezw-berlin.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo gutachten des deutschen bundestags veroeffentlicht</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EZW BerlinUFO-Gutachten des Deutschen Bundestags veröffentlichtDas Gremium hat im November 2009 eine Ausarbeitung vorgelegt mit dem Titel...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2009</span></span></span>

The document is valuable for a different reason: it exposes the weakness of broad official denials. It shows that “the federal government has no UFO files” cannot safely be expanded into “no German authority has ever investigated an unidentified aerial observation”.

## Why missing central files do not prove secrecy

The absence of a national register creates an information gap, but secrecy is only one possible cause and often not the most likely one. Several ordinary administrative processes can remove an event from public view.

A report may have been filed under an unexpected term. “Unidentified light”, “aircraft noise”, “suspected drone”, “radar track”, “balloon” and “airspace violation” can all describe events later discussed as UFO sightings. Searches restricted to the words “UFO” or “UAP” will miss them.

A record may never have been selected for permanent archiving. Government archives do not preserve every operational message, local report or duplicate document. Files can also remain within the creating authority for years before transfer. Even within the Federal Archives, not all holdings are digitised, and catalogue descriptions may identify the responsible office or broad subject rather than every incident contained in a file. The archives advise researchers to use detailed enquiries and finding aids, while their Invenio system provides access only to catalogued holdings that are not subject to search restrictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bundesarchiv.de/en/research-our-records/view-and-use-archive-material/submitting-a-request/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bundesarchiv.de">[Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bundesarchiv.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv Submitting a Request</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stasi-Unterlagen-Archiv Submitting a Request</span></span></span>

Access may also be limited by personal privacy, operational security or intelligence sensitivities. Such restrictions can conceal the details of an event without implying that the hidden material supports an extraordinary explanation. A record of a military radar contact, for example, may be protected because it reveals sensor capabilities rather than because officials believe the object was extraterrestrial.

Finally, some incidents were probably never recorded beyond the witness’s initial contact. A caller who reports a distant light may receive a routine explanation, be referred elsewhere or provide too little information for follow-up. The resulting absence is a failure of evidence preservation, not evidence of suppression.

## What this means for Brandenburg’s UFO history


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Brandenburg’s official record should be treated as a reconstruction rather than a complete case register. A strong investigation begins with the date, time and precise location, then searches outward through police reporting, aviation incidents, military context, archives, local newspapers and private case databases. The closer a sighting was to an airport, restricted area, former military site or known training route, the more important it becomes to search under operational terminology rather than UFO terminology.

</div>

This fragmented structure also limits confident statistics. The number of reports collected by a private group cannot be equated with the number received by government bodies, and neither figure necessarily measures genuinely unexplained events. Duplicate reports may describe the same satellite train or meteor; meanwhile, a potentially important pilot observation may be absent from public UFO databases because it was processed solely as an aviation occurrence.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore modest. Germany did not create a publicly acknowledged, continuous civilian UFO archive comparable to France’s. Relevant Brandenburg material can nevertheless exist across police, aviation, defence, intelligence and archival systems. Missing central files neither demonstrate a government cover-up nor establish that officials conducted a thorough investigation of every report. They show that Germany treated unusual aerial observations as separate administrative problems—and left later researchers to assemble the wider pattern themselves.
