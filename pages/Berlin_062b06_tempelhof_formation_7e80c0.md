## Introduction

The second Tempelhof account describes four white objects crossing the night sky in formation, vanishing, then returning in the opposite direction before one appeared to climb away from the other three. It is more structured than a simple report of unexplained lights, but it is not strong confirmation of the better-known sighting reported from Tempelhof’s control tower earlier that night.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Berlin_062b06_tempelhof_formation_7e80c0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Four Objects illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Berlin_062b06_tempelhof_formation_7e80c0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Berlin_062b06_tempelhof_formation_7e80c0-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

The account came from Airman Third Class Francis J. Wenzel, who was standing outside a building near the air-base fire department at about 11.15pm on 11 August 1952. His statement includes directional sketches, an approximate viewing distance and a clear sequence of movements. Yet he could not determine altitude or speed, heard no sound and supplied no photograph, radar trace or independently signed witness statement. The most defensible judgement is therefore that he probably saw real airborne objects, but that the surviving evidence is insufficient to establish what they were—or whether their apparent reversal and separation reflected unusual performance rather than ordinary aircraft viewed under difficult night-time conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

## The reported flight pattern

Wenzel’s one-page report states that he first saw four objects while standing outside the fire department building. They were flying towards the east and remained visible for roughly one or two minutes. About five minutes after disappearing, they returned, this time travelling west. During the second passage, one object appeared to climb upwards and disappear while the remaining three continued westwards before fading from view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

Wenzel described the objects as white and said that, “in a way”, they resembled the Moon. This wording should not be read as a reliable measurement of size. It may refer only to their whiteness, brightness or circular appearance. He placed them over buildings south-west of the base and estimated that they were about 10 to 15 miles away, while explicitly listing their altitude as undetermined and their speed as unknown. No sound was heard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

That combination is important. A distance estimate without an altitude, angular size or known reference object is highly uncertain at night. The report preserves a number—10 to 15 miles—but offers no method by which Wenzel calculated it. It is best treated as an impression, not a measured range.

He contacted the control tower after the objects appeared for the second time, at approximately 11.20pm. Wenzel wrote that the tower operator also saw them and said that a report would be made. However, the surviving page is Wenzel’s signed account; it does not include a separate signed statement from that tower operator specifically describing the four-object formation. The claimed tower observation therefore adds interest, but it is not equivalent to fully documented independent corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

## How this account differs from the main sighting

The four-object report is often grouped with the principal Tempelhof incident because both occurred close to the same base on the same night. They should nevertheless be treated as separate observations unless stronger evidence emerges that they involved the same objects.

The main sighting began at about 11pm. Control-tower and fire-department personnel described three luminous, cloud-like, elliptical forms south of the airfield. These appeared to hover for approximately 30 seconds. The brightest then moved rapidly east, while the other two disappeared when attention shifted towards it. Some fire-department witnesses thought the effect might have been produced by [searchlights]({{ 'searchlights/' | relative_url }}) on clouds, although they did not see beams rising from the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

Wenzel’s report differs in nearly every descriptive feature:

These differences weaken the idea that Wenzel merely supplied another view of the earlier three lights. His objects behaved more like discrete moving bodies. They crossed the sky, maintained relative spacing and apparently changed formation. The earlier phenomena were diffuse enough for witnesses themselves to compare them with illuminated clouds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

The timing also creates ambiguity rather than clear continuity. The first event was brief, whereas Wenzel’s first passage occurred about 15 minutes later. A connection remains possible: the earlier objects may have moved away and later reappeared, or the stated times may have been approximate. But the file does not demonstrate that connection. Calling the four-object observation “corroboration” risks merging two accounts that differ in number, texture, movement and duration.

The weather recorded at Tempelhof at 11pm included visibility of about 15 miles and broken or partial cloud cover averaging around 7,000 feet. That is relevant to both reports, but in different ways. Clouds could have supported the searchlight explanation proposed for the first sighting. For Wenzel’s formation, however, clouds are more likely to have affected visibility—causing objects to fade, reappear or seem to climb—than to have created four orderly moving points by themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

## What the investigation did—and did not—establish

The [Berlin]({{ 'why-berlins-ufo-history-lives-in/' | relative_url }}) intelligence report shows that the broader incident received more attention than an ordinary civilian letter. Base personnel were interviewed within days, weather records were checked and the radar duty officer was questioned. This reflects Tempelhof’s security significance in divided Berlin: civilian traffic had resumed in 1951, but the site remained under American control and retained an important Cold War aviation role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://corporate.berlin-airport.de/en/company-media/history/berlin-tempelhof-airport.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corporate.berlin-airport.de">[Berlin Airport]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corporate.berlin-airport.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">berlin tempelhof airport</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1951 &#124; Tempelhof Airport is released for civil air and freight traffic by the American occupation forces.1948 &#124; During the Berlin blockad...</span></span></span>

The radar evidence did not confirm the sightings. The duty officer stated that no unidentified objects had been noticed on the CPS-5 radar screen at 11pm. He added that nearby objects might have escaped detection and that unidentified radar returns were common, often being presumed Soviet aircraft when positive identification was impossible. This is not the same as saying radar proved that nothing was there, but it removes a potentially powerful form of corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

For the four-object report in particular, the file contains no recorded interception, aircraft identification check, plotted bearings, measured elevation angles or comparison with flight movements. Nor does it appear to preserve a detailed follow-up interview asking Wenzel how he distinguished a genuine return from a second, unrelated group travelling in the opposite direction.

The lack of such work matters because the most remarkable element—the apparent reversal—is not directly observed as a turn. Wenzel saw objects go east, lose sight of them, and approximately five minutes later saw objects travelling west. He assumed, reasonably but not conclusively, that the second group was the first group returning. Without continuous observation, the record cannot exclude two separate formations.

The archival context also requires caution. Project [Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) files often contain witness reports, questionnaires, correspondence and summary control sheets, but the survival of a document does not mean that investigators validated every statement within it. The National Archives describes these case files as collections of information gathered about sightings, including observers’ accounts and Air Force assessments; they are records of investigation, not certificates that an anomalous event occurred as perceived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

## Conventional explanations that remain possible

No single ordinary explanation can now be proved, largely because the report lacks the measurements and traffic records needed to test one. Several possibilities nevertheless fit the evidence better than an extraordinary craft hypothesis.

**Aircraft flying in formation.** Tempelhof was an active airport and American air base. Four aircraft seen at distance could appear as bright white points, particularly if their forward-facing or landing lights were visible. A formation travelling along one route and another formation later travelling back along it could create the impression of the same objects returning. One aircraft climbing or banking away would match the reported separation without requiring unusual performance.

The absence of sound does not rule this out. If the distance estimate was even approximately correct, aircraft noise could have been weak, delayed, masked by local background sound or directed away from the observer. More importantly, Wenzel did not know the altitude or speed, so the true distance may have differed greatly from his estimate.

**One formation changing heading outside clear view.** The objects may genuinely have reversed course during the five-minute interval, but an ordinary turn could have occurred beyond Wenzel’s field of view. A circuit, holding pattern, training movement or approach-related manoeuvre could produce an outward passage followed by a return. The report provides no bearings precise enough to reconstruct the route against Tempelhof’s traffic pattern.

**Two unrelated groups.** This is less dramatic but methodologically important. The first four lights disappeared; a later group of four lights appeared travelling the other way. The matching number encouraged identification of the two groups as the same objects, yet no continuous observation linked them. At an operating airfield, repeated aircraft movements are an obvious possibility.

**Perspective during a bank or climb.** The object said to rise vertically may have been climbing, but it might also have turned towards or away from the observer. A light changing direction in three-dimensional space can trace a misleading path against a featureless sky. Modern aviation guidance continues to warn that night observation without reliable landmarks makes distance and position difficult to judge, while isolated lights may appear to move or shift because of visual illusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skybrary.aero/index.php/articles/visual-illusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skybrary.aero">[Skybrary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skybrary.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">* Black hole effect: When approaching an airport surrounded by dark terrain with few other lights in the area, pilots can get an illusion...</span></span></span>

**Intermittent cloud obscuration.** With cloud reported around 7,000 feet, aircraft lights could have faded as they entered haze or cloud rather than accelerating away. Wenzel described the objects as blending into the dark sky, not shooting off at visibly extreme speed. That wording is compatible with reduced contrast or changing viewing angle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Project_Blue_Book_report_-_1952-10-8773352-Berlin-Germany.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

Birds are also conceivable in a broad sense, particularly if illuminated from below, but the report offers little support for that solution. The apparent distance, Moon-like brightness, repeated directional passage and disciplined spacing favour aircraft over birds. Balloons fit even less comfortably because a coordinated eastward passage followed by westward travel would require changing winds at different levels or mistaken identification of separate groups.

## A specific report, but a weakly resolved case

The second Tempelhof account deserves attention because it is not merely a vague memory recorded years later. It is a signed contemporary military statement, gives a short chronology and includes formation diagrams. Those features make it one of the more concrete surviving Berlin UFO reports.

They do not, however, make it strong evidence for an exotic vehicle. Its central limitations are severe: one fully documented observer, uncertain distance, unknown altitude and speed, no recorded sound, no photograph, no confirmed radar return and no continuous view of the supposed reversal. Even the tower corroboration survives only through Wenzel’s own account.

Later archival availability has strengthened confidence that the report itself is genuine and that Wenzel gave essentially the description reproduced today. It has not strengthened the underlying claim that four unconventional objects manoeuvred over Tempelhof. No later evidence has supplied the missing traffic records, measurements or independent testimony needed to separate aircraft from a genuinely unexplained formation.

The fairest classification is therefore **unresolved but evidentially weak**. The report preserves an unusual impression made by four lights over a sensitive Cold War airfield. It does not demonstrate extraordinary speed, impossible manoeuvring or a link to the earlier three luminous forms. Its lasting value lies in the contrast: on the same Berlin night, one group of witnesses described cloud-like lights that might have involved searchlights, while another observer reported orderly moving objects that could readily have been aircraft. That difference is a warning against treating every sighting in a single file as one coherent phenomenon.
